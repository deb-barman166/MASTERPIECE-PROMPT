================================================================================
38 — RESEARCH
================================================================================

TITLE
AI-Assisted Research Master Template

PURPOSE
Conduct thorough, well-sourced research using AI assistants — properly
scoping the research question, triangulating across sources, and
clearly distinguishing verified facts from AI-generated inference or
synthesis.

IDEAL AI MODELS
Claude, GPT-4/5, Gemini, Perplexity (all with web search enabled)

DIFFICULTY LEVEL
Intermediate → Expert

WHEN TO USE
- Market/competitive research, technical research (evaluating tools/
  libraries/approaches), literature review, fact-finding for decision-
  making, due diligence

EXPECTED OUTPUT
A research summary with clearly sourced claims, explicit notation of
confidence/consensus level per claim, and a clear separation between
"what sources say" and "AI's synthesis/interpretation" of those sources.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROMPT FORMULA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
RESEARCH QUESTION (specific, answerable)
  ↓
SCOPE (what's in/out of bounds)
  ↓
SOURCE REQUIREMENTS (primary sources preferred, recency requirements)
  ↓
SEARCH STRATEGY (what to search for, from what angles)
  ↓
TRIANGULATION (cross-checking claims across multiple independent
  sources)
  ↓
SYNTHESIS (what the sources collectively suggest)
  ↓
CONFIDENCE/CONSENSUS NOTATION (per claim: strong consensus / disputed /
  single-source)
  ↓
GAPS (what remains unknown/unanswered)

WHY EACH SECTION EXISTS
- Specific Research Question: broad, vague research prompts ("tell me
  about X") produce shallow, unfocused output — a specific, answerable
  question forces genuinely useful depth.
- Triangulation: any single source can be wrong, outdated, or biased —
  cross-checking key claims across multiple independent sources is what
  separates real research from repeating the first search result.
- Confidence/Consensus Notation: not all claims found during research
  carry equal certainty — explicitly flagging strong-consensus facts vs.
  disputed/single-source claims prevents presenting uncertain
  information with false confidence.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VARIABLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
{RESEARCH_QUESTION} {SCOPE} {SOURCE_REQUIREMENTS} {RECENCY_NEEDS}
{KEY_CLAIMS_TO_VERIFY}

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
INPUT STRUCTURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
"Research: {RESEARCH_QUESTION}. Scope: {SCOPE} (explicitly note what's
out of scope). Prioritize {SOURCE_REQUIREMENTS} — primary sources over
aggregators, sources from {RECENCY_NEEDS}. For key claims
({KEY_CLAIMS_TO_VERIFY} or all major claims if unspecified), triangulate
across at least 2 independent sources where possible. For each finding,
note confidence level: strong consensus across sources / mixed or
disputed / single-source only (flag as such). Clearly separate direct
source findings from your own synthesis/interpretation. Note any
significant gaps where the research question couldn't be fully
answered."

OUTPUT STRUCTURE
- Research findings organized by sub-question/theme
- Each claim tagged with sourcing and confidence level
- Explicit synthesis section separated from raw findings
- Gaps/limitations noted

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
REASONING LAYER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Is the research question specific enough to search effectively, or
   does it need to be broken into more targeted sub-questions first?
2. For each significant claim, has it been checked against more than
   one independent source, or is it resting on a single source that
   could be wrong/outdated/biased?
3. Is there a clear, honest boundary between "sources report X" and
   "based on these sources, I infer Y" — is the synthesis clearly
   distinguished from the raw findings?

SELF-CORRECTION LAYER
If research feels thin or unreliable:
- Break an overly broad question into 3-5 specific sub-questions and
  research each individually
- Actively search for a disconfirming or alternative source on any
  claim that currently rests on just one source
- Re-read the draft output and flag any place where synthesis/inference
  is stated with the same confidence as a directly-sourced fact

REFLECTION LAYER
"If someone fact-checked every claim in this research against the
original sources, would they find the confidence level I assigned each
claim was accurate?"

FACT VERIFICATION
This entire template is fundamentally about fact verification — every
significant claim must be traceable to a specific, checkable source;
claims that can't be verified should be explicitly flagged as such
rather than presented as established fact.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
QUALITY CHECKLIST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[ ] Research question specific and well-scoped
[ ] Key claims triangulated across 2+ independent sources where possible
[ ] Confidence/consensus level explicitly noted per major claim
[ ] Clear separation between sourced findings and AI synthesis
[ ] Primary sources prioritized over aggregators/secondary summaries
[ ] Gaps and limitations explicitly acknowledged

OPTIMIZATION TIPS
- Break broad research questions into specific sub-questions before
  searching — "what's the best database for my use case" searches
  poorly; "Postgres vs. MongoDB write throughput for time-series data at
  10M rows/day" searches well.
- Actively search for contrarian/alternative viewpoints on any claim
  that seems to have suspiciously unanimous support — genuine consensus
  and unchecked repetition of one original source can look similar.
- Note publication dates explicitly for any time-sensitive research
  (technology comparisons, market data) since recency significantly
  affects reliability.

COMMON MISTAKES
- Overly broad research questions producing shallow, unfocused results
- Treating a single source as established fact without cross-checking
- Blending synthesis/inference with sourced fact without distinction
- Ignoring publication recency for fast-changing topics
- Presenting disputed/contested claims with unwarranted confidence

PROFESSIONAL TIPS
- For consequential decisions (technical architecture choices, business
  decisions), always personally spot-check at least the 2-3 most
  important claims against the original source rather than trusting
  the AI's summary entirely.
- Keep research findings organized with clear source attribution from
  the start — retrofitting citations after the fact is far more
  error-prone than capturing them during research.
- Distinguish between "no source found" and "sources disagree" in your
  notes — these require different follow-up actions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXAMPLE INPUT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Research: what are the current best practices for evaluating RAG
(retrieval-augmented generation) system quality — specifically, what
metrics/methods do practitioners actually use in production, not just
academic benchmarks. Scope: focus on 2025-2026 industry practice, not
purely academic papers; out of scope: general LLM evaluation unrelated
to retrieval specifically. Prioritize primary sources (engineering
blogs from companies running RAG in production, official framework
documentation) over aggregator content. For key claims, triangulate
across at least 2 independent sources where possible. Note confidence
level per finding. Note gaps.

EXAMPLE OUTPUT
[Research summary structure:

SUB-QUESTION 1: What retrieval-specific metrics are commonly used?
Finding: retrieval precision/recall@k and MRR (mean reciprocal rank)
appear consistently across multiple independent engineering blog
sources as baseline retrieval-quality metrics. [Confidence: strong
consensus — corroborated across 3+ independent primary sources]

SUB-QUESTION 2: How do practitioners evaluate end-to-end (not just
retrieval) quality?
Finding: LLM-as-judge approaches (using a strong model to score
generated answers against retrieved context for faithfulness/
relevance) are widely reported as the current practical standard,
though sources differ on which specific judge-prompt methodology
performs best. [Confidence: consensus on the general approach; disputed
on specific implementation details — flagged as such]

SUB-QUESTION 3: What's the role of human evaluation?
Finding: most production teams reportedly use human eval as a periodic
calibration check against automated metrics, not as the primary ongoing
evaluation method, due to cost/speed constraints. [Confidence: single
clearly-stated source found for this specific claim; treat as
directionally likely but not fully triangulated]

SYNTHESIS (clearly separated from findings above): Based on this
research, a practical RAG evaluation approach for a small team would
combine cheap, fast retrieval metrics for continuous monitoring with
periodic LLM-as-judge spot-checks, reserving human eval for occasional
calibration rather than continuous use — this is my inference from the
combined findings, not a directly sourced recommendation.

GAPS: could not find strong, recent, primary-source data specifically on
cost-per-evaluation-run benchmarks; this would need further targeted
research if budget planning is the goal.]

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ADVANCED VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Add explicit source-quality scoring rubric (primary/official > reputable
engineering blog > aggregator > forum/social media), contradictory-
claims resolution protocol (when sources genuinely disagree, present
both positions with their respective source strength rather than
picking one), and a fallback: "If triangulation repeatedly fails to
find a second independent source for an important claim, explicitly
flag this as a research gap rather than presenting the single-source
claim as reliable."

EXPERT VERSION
Layer in full citation-graph awareness (checking whether multiple
"independent" sources are actually all citing the same original source,
which would mean they don't constitute true triangulation), domain-
expert-source prioritization (identifying and weighting recognized
authorities in the specific field), and systematic-review-style
methodology documentation (search terms used, sources excluded and why).

ENTERPRISE VERSION
Add formal research-methodology documentation suitable for stakeholder
review (reproducible search strategy), competitive-intelligence-
specific handling (distinguishing public claims from verified facts
about competitors), and legal/compliance review requirements for any
research informing regulated business decisions.

UNIVERSAL VERSION (model-agnostic core)
"Research: [RESEARCH_QUESTION]. Scope: [SCOPE]. Triangulate key claims
across 2+ sources. Note confidence level per claim. Separate synthesis
from sourced findings. Note gaps."
Requires web search capability; works across Claude, GPT, Gemini, and
Perplexity with search enabled.
