-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Deliberate Prompting — Slowing Down for High-Stakes Careful Reasoning

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Deliberate prompting is a technique where you explicitly instruct the 
model to SLOW DOWN and reason carefully and thoroughly before 
answering, actively resisting the temptation to jump to a fast, 
"intuitive" first response. It draws on the psychological concept of 
"System 1" (fast, automatic, intuitive thinking) vs. "System 2" (slow, 
effortful, deliberate thinking) — this technique explicitly pushes the 
model toward System 2-style processing by instructing patience, 
thoroughness, and resistance to premature conclusions.

Think of it like the difference between answering a tricky riddle 
with your gut-instinct first guess (which is often wrong because 
riddles are specifically designed to trick fast, intuitive thinking) 
versus deliberately forcing yourself to slow down, question your 
first instinct, and work through it carefully before committing to an 
answer.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Deliberate prompting exists to:
- Counter the model's tendency toward fast, pattern-matched, 
  sometimes-wrong "intuitive" answers on tricky or counter-intuitive 
  problems.
- Improve accuracy specifically on problems DESIGNED to trigger 
  plausible-but-wrong fast answers (classic cognitive bias-style 
  riddles, trick questions, subtly flawed premises).
- Encourage genuine consideration of a question's premise before 
  answering, catching cases where the question itself contains a 
  false assumption or trick.
- Provide an explicit "permission to take time" framing that can 
  improve output quality on complex tasks where thoroughness matters 
  more than speed.
- Serve as an umbrella instruction that can be combined with more 
  specific techniques (Chain-of-Thought, Self-Critique) to reinforce 
  a careful, unhurried reasoning mode.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Trick questions, riddles, or problems specifically designed to 
  trigger a plausible-but-incorrect fast answer (e.g., classic "bat 
  and ball" style cognitive reflection test questions).
- Questions with a subtly flawed or unusual premise that's easy to 
  miss if you answer too quickly.
- High-stakes decisions or analyses where thoroughness is explicitly 
  more valuable than speed.
- Situations where you've noticed the model (or people in general) 
  tend to jump to an intuitive-but-wrong answer on a specific type of 
  problem.
- As a general reinforcement layered on top of Chain-of-Thought or 
  Step-by-Step prompting for extra emphasis on care/thoroughness.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Simple, fast factual lookups where there's no "trick" or complexity 
  to guard against (adds no value, just latency).
- Time-sensitive applications where speed genuinely matters more than 
  marginal accuracy gains.
- Tasks with no meaningful "fast vs. slow thinking" distinction (e.g., 
  purely creative or subjective tasks).

BEST MODELS FOR DELIBERATE PROMPTING:
- OpenAI o-series (o1/o3) and GPT-5-class reasoning modes, Claude with 
  extended thinking, Gemini with Thinking mode, DeepSeek-R1 — these 
  "reasoning models" are BUILT specifically for deliberate, extended 
  reasoning and are the natural best fit for genuinely high-stakes 
  deliberate tasks.
- Standard (non-reasoning) models like base GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 
  (standard mode), and Gemini Pro still benefit meaningfully from 
  explicit "slow down and think carefully" instructions, since they 
  don't reason at length by default.
- Qwen (QwQ/reasoning variants) — strong deliberate reasoning for 
  math/logic-heavy deliberate tasks.
- Mistral / Llama — benefit from explicit deliberate framing, though 
  smaller models have a lower ceiling for how much genuine benefit 
  extended deliberation provides compared to larger/reasoning-native 
  models.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [QUESTION/TASK] + "This question may be trickier than it first 
    appears. Take your time, question your first instinct, and reason 
    through it carefully and thoroughly before answering."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Question/Task — the problem to solve.
    2. Deliberate Framing Instruction — an explicit statement 
       encouraging slow, careful thinking and warning against jumping 
       to a fast first answer.
    3. Premise-Checking Instruction (optional but powerful) — 
       explicitly ask the model to first verify whether the question's 
       premise/assumptions are even correct before answering.
    4. Final Answer — the carefully reasoned conclusion.

Example skeleton:
    "[QUESTION]. 
    This may be trickier than it first appears — resist the urge to 
    answer immediately. First, check whether the question's premise 
    or assumptions are actually correct. Then, reason through the 
    problem carefully and thoroughly. Only after this careful 
    reasoning, provide your final answer."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Explicitly warn that the question "may be trickier than it 
  appears" — this primes more careful processing even before the 
  actual reasoning begins.
- Include an explicit PREMISE-CHECKING step for questions that might 
  contain a flawed assumption — many "trick" questions are trick 
  precisely because of a hidden false premise, not just complex 
  logic.
- Instruct the model to "question your first instinct" directly — 
  this specifically targets the fast, intuitive-answer failure mode.
- Combine with Chain-of-Thought for the actual step-by-step 
  mechanics, using Deliberate Prompting as the "mindset" framing 
  layered on top.
- Reserve this technique for problems where you have genuine reason 
  to suspect a fast answer might be wrong — applying it to every 
  simple question adds unnecessary friction/latency.
- For genuinely critical decisions, consider combining Deliberate 
  Prompting with Self-Consistency (multiple careful, independent 
  reasoning attempts) for maximum reliability.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Applying deliberate framing to simple, non-tricky questions 
  where it adds unnecessary verbosity/latency without any accuracy 
  benefit.
  FIX: Reserve deliberate prompting for problems with a genuine "trick" 
  or complexity that could trigger a wrong fast answer.

- MISTAKE: Not including an explicit premise-checking step, missing 
  cases where the actual "trick" is a false assumption baked into the 
  question itself.
  FIX: Explicitly ask the model to verify the question's premise 
  before attempting to answer it.

- MISTAKE: Using vague deliberate language ("think carefully") without 
  the more pointed "question your first instinct" framing, which is 
  specifically what counters fast, intuitive-but-wrong answers.
  FIX: Use the more targeted phrasing that explicitly names the fast-
  answer failure mode to guard against.

- MISTAKE: Assuming deliberate prompting alone guarantees a correct 
  answer — it improves the ODDS of catching a trick, but doesn't 
  replace genuine domain knowledge or verification for factual 
  correctness.
  FIX: Combine with fact-checking, tool use, or verification steps 
  when factual accuracy (not just logical trickiness) is the concern.

- MISTAKE: Overusing this technique as a blanket instruction across 
  all prompts "just in case," diluting its effectiveness and adding 
  cost.
  FIX: Apply selectively to problems you have genuine reason to treat 
  as potentially tricky or high-stakes.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"A bat and a ball together cost $1.10. The bat costs $1.00 more than 
the ball. How much does the ball cost? 

This may be trickier than it first appears — resist the urge to 
answer immediately with the first number that comes to mind. Reason 
through it carefully before answering."

Expected Output:
Careful reasoning explicitly rejecting the common intuitive-but-wrong 
answer of $0.10 (which would make the bat $1.00, totaling $1.10, but 
that means the bat is only $0.90 more, not $1.00 more), correctly 
setting up the algebra (ball = x, bat = x + 1.00, x + (x+1.00) = 
1.10) to arrive at the correct Final Answer: the ball costs $0.05 
(and the bat costs $1.05, totaling $1.10, with the bat being exactly 
$1.00 more than the ball).

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"If it takes 5 machines 5 minutes to make 5 widgets, how long would 
it take 100 machines to make 100 widgets? 

This question may be trickier than it appears at first glance. First, 
check whether your initial instinct about the relationship between 
machines, time, and widgets is actually correct, then reason through 
it carefully step by step before giving your final answer."

Expected Output:
Reasoning that explicitly resists the tempting-but-wrong intuitive 
leap to "100 minutes," instead correctly identifying that each 
individual machine takes 5 minutes to make 1 widget (since 5 machines 
working in parallel make 5 widgets in 5 minutes, meaning each machine 
makes one widget in that time), so 100 machines working in parallel 
would similarly each make their own widget in the same 5 minutes, 
arriving at the correct Final Answer: 5 minutes.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"I'm designing an evaluation metric for my AI system's response 
quality. My initial idea: 'measure response quality purely by 
response length, since longer, more detailed responses are generally 
more helpful and thorough.' 

This premise may be trickier or flawed in ways that aren't obvious at 
first glance. First, carefully examine whether the underlying 
assumption (that longer = more helpful/thorough) actually holds up 
under scrutiny — consider potential counterexamples or failure modes. 
Then, based on this careful examination, provide your recommendation 
for whether and how to use length as part of a quality metric."

Expected Output:
Careful, deliberate reasoning that resists simply agreeing with the 
premise, instead genuinely examining potential flaws — such as noting 
that length can be trivially gamed (verbose but low-content 
responses would score artificially high), that the IDEAL length is 
actually task-dependent (a factual yes/no question deserves a short 
answer; treating brevity as a failure would be wrong), and that 
length says nothing about ACCURACY or RELEVANCE — leading to a 
carefully reasoned Final Answer/recommendation that length alone is a 
poor standalone quality metric and should, if used at all, be 
combined with task-appropriateness, accuracy, and relevance measures 
rather than treated as a direct proxy for quality — demonstrating 
genuine premise-questioning rather than accepting the flawed initial 
framing.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    [YOUR QUESTION OR TASK]
    
    This may be trickier or more nuanced than it first appears. 
    Resist the urge to answer immediately with your first instinct.
    
    First, check whether the premise or assumptions behind this 
    question/task are actually correct.
    
    Then, reason through the problem carefully and thoroughly, 
    questioning your own initial reasoning as you go.
    
    Only after this careful process, provide your Final Answer.
