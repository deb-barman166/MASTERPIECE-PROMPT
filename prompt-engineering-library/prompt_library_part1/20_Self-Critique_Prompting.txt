20. SELF-CRITIQUE PROMPTING

Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 1: Reasoning)

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Self-Critique Prompting — Systematically Finding Flaws in One's Own 
Output

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Self-Critique prompting is a technique where you instruct the model 
to act as its OWN CRITIC — deliberately searching for weaknesses, 
errors, logical flaws, or unaddressed counterarguments in a response 
it just generated, using a structured, adversarial mindset rather 
than a gentle "review." While Reflection Prompting is a broad "take a 
second look" step, Self-Critique is more POINTED and SYSTEMATIC: it 
specifically tasks the model with actively trying to find fault, 
often using a defined rubric or the perspective of a skeptical expert.

Think of it like the difference between a writer casually rereading 
their essay (Reflection) versus deliberately handing that same essay 
to their harshest, most skeptical critic and asking, "Tear this 
apart — what's wrong with it?" (Self-Critique) — the adversarial 
framing surfaces issues that a gentler review might miss.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Self-Critique prompting exists to:
- Surface WEAKNESSES and FLAWS more aggressively than a passive 
  review would, by adopting a deliberately skeptical/adversarial 
  stance.
- Stress-test arguments, plans, or analyses BEFORE they're finalized 
  or acted upon, catching issues that could be costly if discovered 
  later.
- Reduce overconfidence and one-sidedness in the model's initial 
  output by forcing genuine consideration of counterarguments or edge 
  cases.
- Improve the rigor of persuasive writing, business plans, technical 
  designs, or arguments by identifying the strongest objections BEFORE 
  an external critic does.
- Provide a structured, rubric-based approach to quality assurance 
  that's more systematic than open-ended reflection.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Persuasive writing, arguments, or proposals where you want to 
  anticipate and address counterarguments before publishing/
  presenting.
- Business plans, strategies, or technical designs where finding 
  flaws NOW is far cheaper than finding them after implementation.
- Academic or analytical writing where rigor and addressing 
  counterpoints strengthens credibility.
- Code review or architecture review where an adversarial "what could 
  break this?" mindset catches more issues than a friendly pass.
- Any high-stakes output where you want the model to genuinely 
  "red-team" its own work.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Simple, low-stakes, or purely factual outputs where fault-finding 
  adds no real value.
- Early-stage brainstorming, where excessive self-critique too early 
  can prematurely kill promising but not-yet-refined ideas (save 
  critique for AFTER initial ideation).
- Creative work where the goal is authentic self-expression rather 
  than airtight argumentation (be selective about applying harsh 
  critique to personal creative writing).

BEST MODELS FOR SELF-CRITIQUE:
- Claude — particularly strong at genuine, substantive self-critique, 
  often identifying real structural or logical weaknesses rather than 
  superficial nitpicks.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong at adversarial-style critique, 
  especially for business/strategic and analytical content.
- Gemini Pro — capable self-critique, especially useful for technical/
  scientific claims.
- DeepSeek-R1 / Qwen (reasoning variants) — strong for rigorous 
  self-critique of mathematical/logical arguments and code.
- Mistral / Llama — capable when given a specific critique rubric; 
  benefit from explicit "adopt a skeptical expert persona" framing to 
  produce more substantive (vs. superficial) critique.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [ORIGINAL RESPONSE/ARGUMENT] + "Now critique this as a skeptical 
    [EXPERT/CRITIC]. Identify the [N] strongest weaknesses, flaws, or 
    counterarguments." + [OPTIONAL: "Then address each one."]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Original Content — the response, argument, plan, or work to 
       be critiqued.
    2. Adversarial Framing — explicitly instruct the model to adopt a 
       skeptical, critical stance (often paired with a specific 
       persona, e.g., "a skeptical investor," "a harsh code reviewer," 
       "a devil's advocate").
    3. Structured Critique Request — ask for a specific NUMBER of 
       distinct weaknesses/flaws (e.g., "identify the 3 strongest 
       counterarguments") to force real depth rather than one vague 
       criticism.
    4. (Optional) Rebuttal/Improvement Step — after identifying flaws, 
       ask the model to address or fix each one, closing the loop.

Example skeleton:
    "[ORIGINAL CONTENT]. 
    Now, acting as a skeptical [EXPERT/ROLE], critique this. 
    Identify the [N] strongest weaknesses or flaws, being genuinely 
    critical rather than diplomatic. 
    For each weakness identified, propose a specific fix or 
    counterpoint."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Explicitly ask for a SPECIFIC NUMBER of weaknesses/flaws (e.g., "3 
  strongest counterarguments") — open-ended requests often produce 
  weaker, more generic critique.
- Pair with an adversarial PERSONA (skeptical investor, harsh 
  reviewer, devil's advocate) to push the model past its default 
  tendency toward diplomatic agreeableness.
- Explicitly grant "permission" to be genuinely critical rather than 
  gentle — state something like "don't hold back; be genuinely 
  skeptical" if you notice the model being too soft.
- Follow critique with a REBUTTAL/FIX step so the process results in 
  an improved output, not just a list of problems with nothing done 
  about them.
- Time this AFTER initial idea generation/drafting is complete, not 
  during early brainstorming, to avoid prematurely killing promising 
  raw ideas.
- Use for anything you're about to commit real resources to (money, 
  time, reputation) — the cost of a self-critique pass is trivial 
  compared to catching a costly flaw before it's too late.
- Consider running self-critique from MULTIPLE distinct expert 
  perspectives for especially high-stakes decisions (e.g., critique 
  from a "financial" lens AND a "technical feasibility" lens 
  separately).

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Asking for critique without specifying a number, resulting 
  in a single vague, softened criticism ("this is pretty good, maybe 
  just polish the wording a bit").
  FIX: Explicitly request a specific number of genuine, substantive 
  weaknesses.

- MISTAKE: Not adopting an adversarial persona/framing, leading to an 
  overly agreeable, diplomatic "critique" that doesn't surface real 
  issues.
  FIX: Frame the critique explicitly as coming from a skeptical 
  expert or devil's advocate persona.

- MISTAKE: Applying harsh self-critique too early in the creative/
  ideation process, killing promising rough ideas before they've had 
  a chance to develop.
  FIX: Save self-critique for after an initial draft/plan exists, not 
  during early brainstorming.

- MISTAKE: Generating a list of flaws but never following up with 
  fixes, leaving the work in a worse-feeling but not actually improved 
  state.
  FIX: Always pair critique with a subsequent "now fix each issue" 
  step.

- MISTAKE: Treating a single self-critique pass as fully 
  comprehensive for extremely high-stakes decisions, when multiple 
  critique passes from different angles/personas would surface more 
  issues.
  FIX: For major decisions, consider running self-critique from 
  several distinct expert perspectives.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Here is my argument: 'Schools should ban smartphones entirely 
because they distract students from learning.' 

Now, acting as a skeptical debate opponent, critique this argument. 
Identify the 2 strongest counterarguments, being genuinely critical."

Expected Output:
Two substantive counterarguments, such as: (1) smartphones can also 
serve as valuable educational tools (research, calculators, 
accessibility features) and a total ban discards these benefits along 
with the distractions, and (2) a full ban doesn't address the root 
issue of students' relationship with technology and may simply push 
distraction to other devices or contexts, failing to build the self-
regulation skills students will need long-term.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Here is my startup pitch summary: 'We're building an AI-powered 
resume screening tool that automatically ranks job candidates based 
on resume content, reducing HR screening time by 80%.' 

Now, acting as a skeptical venture capital investor who has seen many 
failed HR-tech startups, critique this pitch. Identify the 3 
strongest weaknesses or risks a real investor would flag, being 
genuinely critical rather than diplomatic. For each weakness, suggest 
how I could address it in my pitch."

Expected Output:
Three substantive investor-grade critiques, such as: (1) algorithmic 
bias/legal risk — automated resume ranking has well-documented 
discrimination risks and potential legal exposure, which the pitch 
doesn't address; (2) differentiation — the HR-tech screening space is 
already crowded with established players, and "80% time reduction" 
alone isn't a strong enough moat; (3) go-to-market — no mention of 
how the company plans to get HR departments (often risk-averse 
buyers) to trust and adopt an AI screening tool — each paired with a 
specific suggestion for strengthening the pitch to address that gap.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Here is my proposed cryptographic vault design: 'BLACKCORE uses a 
dual-file split-secret architecture with XChaCha20-Poly1305 
encryption, Argon2id key derivation, and Shamir Secret Sharing, plus 
ghost/honey vault modes for plausible deniability under coercion.' 

Now, acting as a skeptical, senior security auditor who specializes 
in breaking cryptographic systems, critique this design. Identify the 
3 most significant potential weaknesses or attack vectors a real 
auditor would flag — considering implementation risks, not just 
whether the underlying primitives (XChaCha20-Poly1305, Argon2id, 
Shamir Secret Sharing) are theoretically sound. Be genuinely critical, 
not diplomatic. For each weakness, propose a specific mitigation."

Expected Output:
Three genuinely substantive security-auditor-grade critiques focused 
on IMPLEMENTATION risk rather than just naming the primitives as 
sound, such as: (1) key management/storage — even strong primitives 
fail if key material or Shamir shares are stored insecurely at rest, 
or if the split-secret files are ever backed up together, defeating 
the split-secret purpose; (2) honey/ghost vault detectability — under 
real coercion, forensic analysis of file sizes, metadata, or access 
timestamps could potentially reveal the existence of a hidden real 
vault distinct from the decoy, undermining plausible deniability; (3) 
side-channel risks — Argon2id parameter choices and implementation 
details matter significantly; a naive implementation could still be 
vulnerable to timing or memory-based side-channel attacks despite 
using a theoretically strong KDF — each paired with a specific, 
actionable mitigation (e.g., ensuring split files are never co-
located even in backups, using constant-time operations throughout, 
and carefully tuning Argon2id memory/time parameters against current 
hardware capabilities).

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Here is my [ARGUMENT/PLAN/DESIGN/PITCH]: [PASTE CONTENT]
    
    Now, acting as a skeptical [EXPERT ROLE/PERSONA — e.g., 
    investor, security auditor, senior code reviewer, devil's 
    advocate], critique this. 
    
    Identify the [SPECIFIC NUMBER, e.g., 3] strongest weaknesses, 
    flaws, or risks — be genuinely critical, not diplomatic.
    
    For each weakness identified, propose a specific fix or 
    mitigation.
