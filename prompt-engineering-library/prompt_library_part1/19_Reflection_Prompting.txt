19. REFLECTION PROMPTING
=====================================================================
Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 1: Reasoning)
Author: Deb Barman
=====================================================================

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Reflection Prompting — Reviewing and Learning From Prior Output

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Reflection prompting is a technique where you instruct the model to 
generate an initial response, and then explicitly REFLECT on that 
response — reviewing it for errors, gaps, or weaknesses — before 
either confirming it's correct or producing an improved version. 
Unlike Self-Critique (which focuses specifically on finding faults) 
or Self-Refine (which focuses on the improvement loop), Reflection 
Prompting is the broader practice of having the model pause and think 
ABOUT its own output — asking "was that actually right? What did I 
maybe miss? What would I do differently?" — as a distinct, deliberate 
step.

Think of it like a writer who finishes a first draft, then instead of 
immediately submitting it, takes a moment to reread it and honestly 
ask: "Does this actually make sense? Did I address everything I meant 
to?" — before deciding it's ready to send.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Reflection prompting exists to:
- Catch errors, omissions, or weak reasoning that the model's INITIAL 
  response might contain, before that response is treated as final.
- Improve overall output quality by adding a deliberate "second look" 
  step, mimicking how skilled humans review their own work.
- Build in a natural checkpoint for identifying whether a response 
  fully addressed all parts of a complex or multi-part request.
- Serve as the conceptual foundation that more specific techniques 
  (Self-Critique, Self-Refine) build upon and specialize.
- Improve the model's calibration — recognizing when it's actually 
  uncertain about something rather than presenting everything with 
  equal confidence.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Complex, multi-part tasks where it's easy to accidentally miss or 
  under-address one component.
- Any task where a "second look" plausibly catches meaningful errors 
  (writing, analysis, code, planning).
- Situations where you want the model to explicitly acknowledge 
  uncertainty or limitations in its own answer, rather than presenting 
  everything with false confidence.
- Higher-stakes outputs where an extra reflection pass is worth the 
  added time/tokens.
- As a general-purpose "quality gate" you can append to almost any 
  other prompting technique.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Very simple, low-stakes tasks where a reflection step adds latency/
  cost without meaningful benefit (e.g., "what's 7 x 8?").
- Time-sensitive situations where getting SOME answer quickly matters 
  more than an extra polish pass.
- When reflection has already been performed via a more specific 
  technique (Self-Critique, Self-Refine) — avoid redundant, stacked 
  reflection steps that don't add new value.

BEST MODELS FOR REFLECTION PROMPTING:
- Claude — particularly strong and well-calibrated at genuine self-
  reflection, often identifying real limitations or gaps rather than 
  just performatively agreeing its first answer was already perfect.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong reflective capability, especially for 
  identifying missed requirements in complex, multi-part prompts.
- Gemini Pro — capable reflection, particularly useful for reviewing 
  structured/technical outputs.
- DeepSeek-R1 / Qwen (reasoning variants) — naturally incorporate 
  reflection-like behavior as part of their extended reasoning 
  process.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of reflection when explicitly prompted; 
  smaller models sometimes produce more superficial reflection 
  ("yes, this looks good!") and benefit from being asked pointed, 
  specific reflection questions rather than an open-ended "review 
  your answer."

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [TASK] → [INITIAL RESPONSE] → "Now reflect on your response: 
    Did you address everything? Are there any errors, gaps, or 
    unclear parts?" → [REVISED/CONFIRMED RESPONSE]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Original Task — the task to complete.
    2. Initial Response Generation — the model's first attempt.
    3. Explicit Reflection Trigger — a direct instruction to review 
       the response against specific criteria (accuracy, 
       completeness, clarity).
    4. Reflection Output — the model's honest assessment of its own 
       response, including any identified issues.
    5. Final Response — either a confirmation that the original was 
       sound, or a revised response addressing identified issues.

Example skeleton:
    "[TASK]. 
    
    [Model generates initial response] 
    
    Now, reflect on your response above: 
    - Did you fully address every part of the request? 
    - Are there any factual errors or unclear explanations? 
    - What would you improve if you had one more pass? 
    
    Based on this reflection, provide your final, refined response."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Give the reflection step SPECIFIC criteria to check against 
  (completeness, accuracy, clarity, tone) rather than a vague "review 
  your answer," which tends to produce more genuine, useful 
  self-assessment.
- Explicitly ask the model to identify what it would IMPROVE, not 
  just whether the answer was "good" — this pushes past superficial 
  self-approval.
- For multi-part requests, explicitly ask the reflection step to 
  verify EACH part of the original request was addressed (a mini-
  checklist approach).
- Encourage honest uncertainty acknowledgment — explicitly state 
  it's okay (and valuable) for the reflection to say "I'm not fully 
  confident about X part of this."
- Use reflection selectively for tasks where it adds genuine value, 
  not as a blanket step for every single response (this adds 
  unnecessary latency/cost at scale).
- Combine with Self-Critique for a more structured, systematic fault-
  finding process, or with Self-Refine when you specifically want an 
  improvement LOOP (multiple reflect-and-improve cycles) rather than 
  a single reflection pass.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Asking a vague "review your answer" without specific 
  criteria, leading to shallow, non-substantive reflection ("Yes, 
  this looks good!").
  FIX: Provide specific things to check (completeness, accuracy, 
  clarity, tone, format).

- MISTAKE: Treating reflection as purely performative — the model 
  says it reflected but doesn't actually change anything even when 
  real issues exist.
  FIX: Explicitly ask "what SPECIFIC change would improve this?" 
  rather than a yes/no "is this good?"

- MISTAKE: Using reflection on every single simple task, adding 
  unnecessary cost/latency for no real benefit.
  FIX: Reserve reflection for complex, multi-part, or higher-stakes 
  tasks.

- MISTAKE: Not giving the model permission/encouragement to express 
  genuine uncertainty, leading to overconfident reflection that 
  misses real limitations.
  FIX: Explicitly state that acknowledging uncertainty or 
  limitations is valued and expected where genuinely present.

- MISTAKE: Stacking multiple redundant reflection steps (reflection + 
  self-critique + self-refine all applied to the same output without 
  clear distinct purpose) which wastes tokens without proportional 
  benefit.
  FIX: Choose ONE reflection-family technique suited to your specific 
  need, rather than layering all of them by default.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Explain the difference between a Python list and a Python tuple. 

[After the initial explanation] 

Now reflect: Did your explanation cover both the KEY similarities and 
differences? Is there anything important you left out? Provide your 
final, refined explanation."

Expected Output:
An initial explanation covering mutability as the main difference, 
followed by genuine reflection noting perhaps performance 
implications or use-case guidance were initially omitted, then a 
refined final explanation incorporating that additional point (e.g., 
tuples being slightly more memory-efficient and appropriate for fixed 
collections, lists better for collections that need to change).

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a 200-word explanation of how RAG (Retrieval-Augmented 
Generation) works, suitable for someone with basic Python knowledge 
but no ML background. 

[After the initial explanation] 

Now reflect on your explanation: 
- Did you avoid unexplained jargon given the stated audience? 
- Did you cover the full RAG pipeline (retrieval AND generation), or 
  did you focus too heavily on one part? 
- Is the word count close to the requested 200 words? 

Provide your final, refined explanation based on this reflection."

Expected Output:
An initial explanation, followed by honest reflection potentially 
identifying an unexplained term (e.g., "embedding" used without 
definition) or noting the explanation leaned too heavily on the 
retrieval step without adequately covering how retrieved content 
integrates into generation, followed by a refined final explanation 
that fixes the identified gaps and confirms word count is 
appropriately close to 200.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"I'm designing the 'God Mode pipeline' for my 100-agent swarm system 
(built with Google ADK and Gemini 2.0 Flash). Propose an architecture 
for how a top-level 'God Mode' controller could override or 
coordinate individual agent decisions in emergency/critical 
situations, while normally letting agents operate autonomously. 

[After the initial architecture proposal] 

Now reflect critically on your own proposal: 
- Does it fully address BOTH the 'normal autonomous operation' mode 
  AND the 'emergency override' mode, or did you focus too heavily on 
  one? 
- Are there any failure modes or edge cases in your proposed design 
  that you didn't consider (e.g., what happens if the God Mode 
  controller itself fails)? 
- Is there a single-point-of-failure risk in what you proposed? 
- What would you genuinely change if you had one more design pass? 

Based on this honest reflection, provide your final, refined 
architecture proposal."

Expected Output:
An initial architecture proposal, followed by genuinely substantive 
reflection (e.g., honestly identifying that the initial design 
created the God Mode controller as a single point of failure with no 
fallback, or that normal autonomous operation wasn't clearly 
specified in terms of HOW agents know they're NOT in override mode), 
followed by a meaningfully revised final proposal that addresses the 
identified gap (e.g., adding a heartbeat/failover mechanism so agents 
revert to pre-defined safe autonomous behavior if the God Mode 
controller becomes unresponsive) — demonstrating genuine, substantive 
self-reflection rather than superficial confirmation.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    [YOUR ORIGINAL TASK]
    
    [Model provides initial response]
    
    Now reflect honestly on your response above:
    - Did you fully address every part of the original request?
    - Are there any factual errors, gaps, or unclear explanations?
    - Are there any edge cases, limitations, or uncertainties you 
      should acknowledge?
    - What specifically would you improve with one more pass?
    
    Based on this reflection, provide your final, refined response.
