-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Debate Prompting — Arriving at Truth Through Structured Argumentation

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Debate prompting is a multi-agent technique where TWO (or more) 
distinct "agent" perspectives are instructed to argue OPPOSING sides 
of a question, proposal, or claim, taking turns responding to and 
challenging each other's points — with the goal of surfacing a more 
thorough, well-tested understanding than either single perspective 
would produce alone. Often, a third neutral "Judge" role synthesizes 
the debate into a balanced final conclusion, weighing which arguments 
held up best under challenge.

Think of it like a courtroom: rather than a single lawyer presenting 
one side and calling it settled, you have a Prosecution and a Defense 
each rigorously arguing their position, cross-examining the other's 
claims, with a Judge (or jury) ultimately weighing the strength of 
both sides' surviving arguments — this adversarial structure tends to 
surface weaknesses and considerations that a single, one-sided 
analysis would miss.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Debate prompting exists to:
- Surface the STRONGEST arguments on multiple sides of a genuinely 
  contested question, rather than settling for a single, possibly 
  one-sided analysis.
- Stress-test a proposal, decision, or claim by having it actively 
  challenged and defended, revealing weaknesses that a non-adversarial 
  analysis might miss.
- Reduce the risk of the model defaulting to a single "safe" or 
  generic answer on complex, genuinely debatable topics by forcing 
  genuine engagement with counterarguments.
- Provide a structured, balanced way to explore controversial or 
  multi-sided issues without the model needing to prematurely commit 
  to one "correct" position.
- Improve decision-making on genuinely close calls by making the 
  trade-offs and strongest objections to each option explicit before 
  a final judgment is made.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Genuinely contested questions or decisions with legitimate arguments 
  on multiple sides (business strategy trade-offs, technical 
  architecture choices, policy questions, ethical dilemmas).
- Evaluating a proposal or plan where you want to see it seriously 
  challenged before committing to it.
- Exploring a topic from multiple angles for educational or research 
  purposes, ensuring a fair, comprehensive treatment.
- Situations where a single-perspective analysis risks being one-
  sided, biased, or missing important counterarguments.
- Any decision where seeing the STRONGEST version of opposing views 
  would meaningfully improve your confidence in the final choice.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Questions with a single objectively correct answer (e.g., factual 
  or mathematical questions) — debate adds no value when there's 
  nothing genuinely contestable.
- Simple, low-stakes decisions where the overhead of structured debate 
  isn't justified by the decision's importance.
- Topics where manufacturing "both sides" would be misleading because 
  one position is essentially settled/overwhelming (avoid false-
  balance debates on settled factual matters).

BEST MODELS FOR DEBATE PROMPTING:
- Claude — excellent at genuinely representing DIFFERENT perspectives 
  with real intellectual honesty and nuance, rather than creating a 
  weak strawman for one side.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong at constructing substantive arguments 
  for multiple positions and engaging in genuine back-and-forth 
  rebuttal.
- Gemini Pro — capable of structured multi-perspective debate, 
  particularly for technical/business decision contexts.
- Grok — well-suited to spirited, direct debate-style engagement.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — capable for technical debates (e.g., architecture 
  trade-off debates) though may show less nuance on softer, more 
  subjective topics compared to top-tier general models.
- IMPORTANT: For genuinely rigorous debates, some practitioners use 
  TWO DIFFERENT models (e.g., Claude arguing one side, GPT arguing 
  the other) to reduce the risk of a single model's inherent biases 
  affecting both "sides" of the debate equally.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    "Debate this question: [QUESTION/PROPOSAL]. 
    Position A: Argue FOR [X]. 
    Position B: Argue AGAINST [X] (or FOR the alternative). 
    Have each position respond to the other's strongest points across 
    [N] rounds. 
    Then, as a neutral Judge, synthesize which arguments held up best 
    and provide a balanced conclusion."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Debate Topic/Question — the contested claim, proposal, or 
       decision to be debated.
    2. Position Definitions — clearly define what each side is 
       arguing (Position A vs. Position B, or multiple distinct 
       positions).
    3. Round Structure — define how many rounds of argument/rebuttal 
       will occur (e.g., "3 rounds: opening argument, rebuttal, 
       closing argument").
    4. Engagement Requirement — instruct each position to genuinely 
       ENGAGE with and respond to the other's specific points, not 
       just restate their own position repeatedly.
    5. Judge/Synthesis Role — a neutral final step that weighs the 
       strongest surviving arguments from each side and produces a 
       balanced conclusion (not necessarily declaring one side the 
       "winner," but synthesizing genuine insight from the exchange).

Example skeleton:
    "Debate: [QUESTION]. 
    Position A: [FIRST STANCE]. 
    Position B: [SECOND STANCE]. 
    
    Round 1 — Opening arguments: 
    Position A: [argument] 
    Position B: [argument] 
    
    Round 2 — Rebuttals (each position must directly respond to the 
    other's Round 1 argument): 
    Position A: [rebuttal] 
    Position B: [rebuttal] 
    
    Judge: Synthesize the strongest points from both sides and 
    provide a balanced final conclusion."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Require GENUINE ENGAGEMENT — each round's argument must specifically 
  respond to the opposing side's PREVIOUS point, not just restate the 
  same original position in different words.
- Set a defined NUMBER OF ROUNDS (2-4 is typically sufficient) to keep 
  the debate focused and prevent it from running indefinitely.
- Instruct each position to argue its STRONGEST possible case — a 
  weak, strawman version of one side defeats the entire purpose of 
  this technique.
- Include a neutral JUDGE/synthesis step at the end — the value of a 
  debate often comes from the final synthesis of what genuinely held 
  up under challenge, not just the raw arguments themselves.
- For genuinely important decisions, consider using this technique 
  with real diversity in the "arguing" perspectives (different 
  underlying models, or explicitly instructing very different starting 
  assumptions/values for each side).
- Avoid forcing artificial "both sides" framing on settled factual 
  questions — reserve debate for genuinely contested matters where 
  legitimate disagreement exists.
- The Judge role should explicitly note WHERE each side made a 
  stronger point, rather than giving a vague "both sides have merit" 
  non-conclusion.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Each position just restating its opening argument in later 
  rounds instead of genuinely responding to the other side's specific 
  points.
  FIX: Explicitly require each round to directly address the 
  opponent's previous argument.

- MISTAKE: Creating a strawman/weak version of one side to make the 
  other side look better, defeating the purpose of genuine stress-
  testing.
  FIX: Explicitly instruct each position to argue its strongest 
  possible case.

- MISTAKE: No defined stopping point, letting the debate continue 
  indefinitely without converging toward useful synthesis.
  FIX: Set an explicit number of rounds.

- MISTAKE: Skipping the Judge/synthesis step, leaving the raw 
  arguments without any actionable conclusion or insight.
  FIX: Always include a neutral synthesis step that weighs which 
  points genuinely held up.

- MISTAKE: Using debate to manufacture false balance on settled 
  factual questions where one position is simply, verifiably correct.
  FIX: Reserve debate for genuinely contested matters with legitimate 
  substance on multiple sides.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Debate: Should students be allowed to use AI tools like ChatGPT for 
homework? 
Position A: Argue that students SHOULD be allowed to use AI tools for 
homework. 
Position B: Argue that students should NOT be allowed to use AI tools 
for homework. 

Round 1 — Opening arguments from each position. 
Round 2 — Each position must directly respond to the other's Round 1 
argument. 

Judge: Synthesize the strongest points from both sides into a 
balanced conclusion."

Expected Output:
Position A's opening argument (e.g., AI as a learning aid similar to 
calculators, preparing students for a future where AI literacy 
matters), Position B's opening argument (e.g., risk of undermining 
genuine skill development and critical thinking), followed by genuine 
Round 2 rebuttals directly engaging each other's specific points, and 
a Judge synthesis that identifies, for example, that the real crux is 
HOW AI is used (as a crutch to bypass learning vs. as a tool 
alongside genuine effort) rather than a simple yes/no, offering a 
nuanced, balanced conclusion.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Debate: For a solo developer building a personal AI project, is it 
better to build on established frameworks (like LangChain/LangGraph) 
or build custom infrastructure from scratch? 
Position A: Argue FOR using established frameworks. 
Position B: Argue FOR building custom infrastructure from scratch. 

Round 1 — Opening arguments. 
Round 2 — Rebuttals, directly addressing the opposing side's specific 
points. 
Round 3 — Closing arguments, each acknowledging the strongest point 
made by the OTHER side before restating their position. 

Judge: Provide a balanced conclusion, noting what factors would tip 
the decision one way or the other."

Expected Output:
Position A arguing established frameworks save development time, 
benefit from community support/battle-testing, and reduce maintenance 
burden; Position B arguing custom infrastructure offers full control, 
avoids framework limitations/bloat, and provides deeper learning value 
for a developer building expertise; genuine Round 2 rebuttals (e.g., 
Position A challenging that "full control" often isn't needed and 
custom code becomes a maintenance burden of its own; Position B 
challenging that frameworks can become black boxes that limit deep 
understanding); Round 3 closings that each genuinely acknowledge the 
other's strongest point; and a Judge synthesis noting the decision 
likely depends on factors like the project's complexity, the 
developer's learning goals, and long-term maintenance plans — 
providing genuinely useful decision criteria rather than a flat 
verdict.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Debate: Should an AI-powered content moderation system prioritize 
minimizing false positives (wrongly flagging safe content) or 
minimizing false negatives (missing genuinely harmful content), given 
that optimizing heavily for one typically worsens the other? 
Position A: Argue that minimizing FALSE POSITIVES should be the 
priority. 
Position B: Argue that minimizing FALSE NEGATIVES should be the 
priority. 

Round 1 — Opening arguments, each grounded in the real trade-offs and 
stakeholder impacts involved. 
Round 2 — Rebuttals, directly engaging with the specific real-world 
harms/costs the other side raised. 
Round 3 — Closing arguments, each proposing a scenario/context where 
their priority would clearly be correct, and acknowledging a scenario 
where the OTHER side's priority would be correct instead. 

Judge: Rather than declaring one side an absolute winner, synthesize 
the debate into a framework for WHEN each priority should take 
precedence, based on the strongest points raised by both sides."

Expected Output:
Position A grounding its argument in real costs of false positives 
(legitimate speech suppressed, user trust erosion, chilling effects 
on expression); Position B grounding its argument in real costs of 
false negatives (genuine harm reaching users, platform liability, 
safety failures); genuine engagement in Round 2 with the SPECIFIC 
harms raised by the other side rather than generic restatement; Round 
3 closings that each identify a context favoring their own priority 
(e.g., Position A citing educational/discussion platforms where 
false positives are especially costly to discourse; Position B citing 
platforms serving vulnerable populations like minors where false 
negatives carry severe risk) while genuinely acknowledging the other's 
valid context; and a Judge synthesis that produces a genuinely useful 
decision framework (e.g., noting that severity/reversibility of 
potential harm, the vulnerability of the affected user base, and the 
availability of human review as a backstop are the key factors that 
should determine which priority takes precedence in a given context) 
rather than a simplistic single verdict — demonstrating the real value 
of structured debate on a genuinely nuanced trade-off.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Debate: [YOUR CONTESTED QUESTION, PROPOSAL, OR DECISION]
    
    Position A: Argue FOR [STANCE A]
    Position B: Argue FOR [STANCE B / AGAINST STANCE A]
    
    Round 1 — Opening arguments from each position.
    Round 2 — Rebuttals: each position must directly respond to the 
    other's specific Round 1 points.
    Round 3 (optional) — Closing arguments: each position acknowledges 
    the strongest point made by the OTHER side before restating its 
    conclusion.
    
    Judge: As a neutral party, synthesize which arguments held up 
    best under challenge, and provide a balanced conclusion — 
    including, if relevant, the conditions under which each side's 
    position would be the stronger choice.
