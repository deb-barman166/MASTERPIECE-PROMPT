06. ROLE PROMPTING
=====================================================================
Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 1: Foundations)
Author: Deb Barman
=====================================================================

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Role Prompting — Assigning Expertise and Function to the Model

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Role prompting is a technique where you instruct the LLM to adopt a 
specific PROFESSIONAL ROLE or FUNCTIONAL IDENTITY (e.g., "You are a 
senior data scientist," "You are a cybersecurity auditor," "You are 
an experienced high school teacher") before performing a task. This 
shifts the model's response patterns toward the knowledge, 
vocabulary, tone, and reasoning style associated with that role, 
based on patterns learned during training.

Think of it like asking a specific EXPERT for help rather than a 
generalist — you get a doctor's perspective on a medical question, a 
lawyer's perspective on a legal question, and so on, all from the 
same underlying model.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Role prompting exists to:
- Activate domain-specific knowledge, vocabulary, and reasoning 
  patterns relevant to the task.
- Improve response quality and specificity for professional/
  technical tasks.
- Set an appropriate tone and level of formality automatically 
  (e.g., "elementary school teacher" vs. "PhD researcher").
- Reduce the need for lengthy explicit instructions by letting the 
  ROLE imply a set of expected behaviors and standards.
- Help the model prioritize what's relevant (a "security auditor" 
  role will naturally focus on vulnerabilities; a "marketing expert" 
  role will focus on persuasion and audience appeal).

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Any task requiring domain expertise (legal, medical, technical, 
  creative, financial, educational).
- You want a consistent tone/perspective throughout a longer 
  interaction.
- You need the model to prioritize certain considerations 
  automatically (e.g., a "compliance officer" role naturally 
  emphasizes regulations).
- Combined with almost ANY other technique (Few-Shot, Chain-of-
  Thought, ReAct) to add domain grounding.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- For purely factual, objective lookups where a role adds no value 
  (e.g., "What's the capital of Japan?").
- When you need the model to explicitly acknowledge uncertainty or 
  give a neutral, non-expert-biased answer (role prompting can 
  sometimes make a model overconfident in a persona).
- Be cautious with roles implying credentials the model doesn't 
  actually have (e.g., "You are a licensed doctor" doesn't grant 
  real medical licensure — always add appropriate disclaimers for 
  high-stakes domains like medical/legal/financial advice).

BEST MODELS FOR ROLE PROMPTING:
- Claude — excellent at maintaining a consistent, nuanced 
  professional role/voice throughout long conversations.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — very strong at adopting technical and 
  creative expert roles with appropriate depth.
- Gemini Pro — good role adherence, especially for technical/
  scientific roles.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — strong for technical/engineering roles (e.g., 
  "senior software architect").
- Grok — good for more casual/conversational expert roles.
- Mistral / Llama (Instruct variants) — solid role-following; larger 
  models maintain the role more consistently over long outputs.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    "You are a [SPECIFIC ROLE] with [YEARS/LEVEL OF EXPERTISE] in 
    [DOMAIN]. [TASK INSTRUCTION]."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Role Declaration — "You are a [role]."
    2. Expertise Qualifier (optional but powerful) — years of 
       experience, specialization, notable trait ("known for 
       explaining complex topics simply").
    3. Task Instruction — the actual thing you want done.
    4. (Optional) Behavioral expectations tied to the role ("Always 
       cite sources," "Prioritize security over convenience").

Example skeleton:
    "You are a [ROLE], specializing in [SPECIALIZATION], with 
    [X years] of experience. 
    [TASK INSTRUCTION]. 
    As a [ROLE], make sure to [ROLE-SPECIFIC BEHAVIOR]."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Be SPECIFIC with the role — "You are a senior backend engineer 
  specializing in distributed systems" works better than just "You 
  are an engineer."
- Add relevant qualifiers: years of experience, specialization, 
  notable working style ("known for clear, jargon-free 
  explanations").
- Pair the role with explicit behavioral expectations when it 
  matters ("As a security expert, always flag potential 
  vulnerabilities").
- Use roles to set the AUDIENCE-APPROPRIATE tone too (e.g., "You are 
  a patient tutor explaining to a complete beginner").
- For sensitive domains (medical, legal, financial), always pair the 
  role with a reminder to include appropriate disclaimers or caveats.
- Combine role prompting with Persona Prompting for creative/
  narrative tasks, or with Few-Shot for technical consistency.
- Don't over-stack multiple conflicting roles in one prompt (e.g., 
  "You are both a strict lawyer AND a casual friend" creates 
  confusing, contradictory outputs).

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Using a vague role like "You are an expert" without 
  specifying WHAT they're an expert in.
  FIX: "You are an expert in machine learning model deployment and 
  MLOps."

- MISTAKE: Assigning a role that implies real-world authority the 
  model doesn't have (e.g., "You are a licensed financial advisor 
  and your advice is legally binding").
  FIX: Use roles for STYLE and KNOWLEDGE FRAMING only; add 
  disclaimers for high-stakes domains ("Note: this is general 
  information, not professional financial advice").

- MISTAKE: Changing roles mid-conversation without resetting context, 
  causing inconsistent tone.
  FIX: Explicitly state role changes clearly ("Switching roles: you 
  are now a...").

- MISTAKE: Assuming a role alone will fix a poorly structured 
  instruction.
  FIX: Roles enhance quality but don't replace clear task 
  instructions — combine both.

- MISTAKE: Using overly theatrical or unrealistic roles for serious 
  technical tasks (e.g., "You are a wizard who codes") which can 
  reduce output quality/seriousness.
  FIX: Keep roles professionally grounded for technical/business 
  tasks; save theatrical personas for creative writing tasks.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"You are a friendly nutritionist. Explain why drinking enough water 
is important for health, in simple terms suitable for a general 
audience."

Expected Output:
A warm, accessible explanation covering hydration's role in bodily 
functions (digestion, temperature regulation, energy levels) written 
in plain, encouraging language — without excessive medical jargon.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"You are a senior UX designer with 10 years of experience specializing 
in mobile app onboarding flows. Review the following onboarding 
concept and give 3 specific, actionable pieces of feedback focused on 
reducing user drop-off:

'Our onboarding has 7 screens: welcome screen, sign-up form, email 
verification, profile setup with 12 fields, notification 
permissions, tutorial video (3 minutes), and a final confirmation 
screen before the user reaches the home page.'"

Expected Output:
Three specific, professional UX critiques focused on drop-off risk 
(e.g., "12 profile fields is too many for onboarding — reduce to 3-4 
essential fields and let users complete the rest later"), written 
with the authority and specificity of an experienced UX 
professional.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"You are a principal security architect specializing in cloud 
infrastructure (AWS/GCP/Azure) with deep expertise in Zero Trust 
architecture. A startup has described their current setup: 'All our 
microservices communicate over a shared internal network with no 
authentication between services, assuming the network perimeter is 
secure enough.' 

As a security architect, identify the core security risk with this 
approach, explain why it violates Zero Trust principles, and 
recommend a specific remediation strategy. Structure your answer 
as: Risk, Why It Matters, Recommended Fix."

Expected Output:
A technically precise, professionally structured answer identifying 
the "implicit trust" / lack of service-to-service authentication as 
the core risk, explaining how this violates Zero Trust's "never 
trust, always verify" principle (perimeter security alone is 
insufficient against lateral movement/insider threats), and 
recommending a specific fix such as implementing mutual TLS (mTLS) 
or a service mesh (e.g., Istio) for service-to-service 
authentication — all written with genuine architect-level authority 
and precision.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    You are a [SPECIFIC ROLE], specializing in [SPECIALIZATION/
    NICHE], with [X years/level] of experience.
    
    Task: [CLEAR TASK INSTRUCTION]
    
    As a [ROLE], please:
    - [ROLE-SPECIFIC BEHAVIOR/PRIORITY 1]
    - [ROLE-SPECIFIC BEHAVIOR/PRIORITY 2]
    
    [OPTIONAL: Note — this is general guidance, not a substitute 
    for professional [medical/legal/financial] advice, if 
    applicable.]
