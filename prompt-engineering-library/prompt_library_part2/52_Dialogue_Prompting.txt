52. DIALOGUE PROMPTING
=====================================================================
Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 2: Creative)
Author: Deb Barman

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Dialogue Prompting — Writing Authentic, Purposeful Character 
Conversation

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Dialogue prompting is a technique focused specifically on generating 
CONVERSATIONAL EXCHANGES between characters that feel authentic and 
naturalistic while also doing genuine dramatic WORK within a narrative 
— revealing character, advancing plot, creating tension, or conveying 
information — rather than dialogue that's purely functional/expository 
("As you know, Bob...") or generically pleasant with no real purpose. 
This applies across prose fiction, screenplays, game narrative, and 
any other format where characters need to speak to each other 
convincingly, building on the character foundations established via 
Character Prompting (file 50).

Think of it like the difference between two people at a party making 
pure small talk with no subtext or purpose (technically realistic but 
dramatically inert) versus a scripted conversation where every line 
does double duty — sounding natural while also revealing something 
about who's speaking, escalating tension, or moving the story forward 
— professional dialogue writing is fundamentally about this kind of 
efficient, purposeful naturalism.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Dialogue prompting exists to:
- Produce conversation that sounds AUTHENTICALLY human/naturalistic — 
  with interruptions, subtext, imperfect grammar, and the specific 
  cadences of real speech — rather than overly formal, complete-sentence 
  prose dialogue.
- Ensure dialogue does genuine DRAMATIC WORK (revealing character, 
  building tension, conveying necessary information) rather than being 
  purely decorative or filler.
- Support SUBTEXT — the gap between what characters say and what they 
  actually mean/want, which is often what makes dialogue feel 
  sophisticated rather than on-the-nose.
- Maintain DISTINCT VOICES for different characters speaking in the 
  same scene, so dialogue is identifiable even without speaker tags.
- Avoid common dialogue pitfalls: exposition dumps disguised as 
  conversation, characters who all sound identical, and dialogue that 
  simply states emotions rather than revealing them through what's 
  said and left unsaid.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Writing conversational scenes in fiction, screenplays, or game 
  narrative.
- Developing dialogue-heavy content where character voice 
  differentiation and subtext matter.
- Revising existing dialogue that feels flat, expository, or overly 
  formal to make it more naturalistic.
- Practice/exercise writing focused specifically on dialogue craft.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Non-conversational narrative content (pure description, internal 
  monologue without spoken exchange).
- Purely functional/transactional exchanges with no dramatic purpose 
  (a character ordering coffee with zero subtext or character 
  revelation needed) — not every line of dialogue needs heavy 
  dramatic weight, so don't force it where genuinely unnecessary.
- Technical/instructional content that happens to be in Q&A format but 
  isn't actually character dialogue.

BEST MODELS FOR DIALOGUE PROMPTING:
- Claude — particularly strong at naturalistic dialogue with genuine 
  subtext, generally avoiding overly expository or on-the-nose 
  emotional statements in favor of more sophisticated, implication-
  driven exchanges.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong dialogue generation, versatile across 
  tones and genres.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra — capable dialogue writing, solid general 
  performance.
- Grok — capable of punchy, distinctive, often more irreverent 
  dialogue styles.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of basic naturalistic dialogue; may need 
  more explicit prompting toward subtext and away from on-the-nose 
  emotional statements, especially in smaller model variants.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [SCENE CONTEXT] + [CHARACTERS INVOLVED + THEIR ESTABLISHED VOICES] 
    + [WHAT EACH CHARACTER WANTS FROM THIS CONVERSATION] + [WHAT'S NOT 
    BEING SAID DIRECTLY/SUBTEXT] + [DRAMATIC PURPOSE OF THE SCENE]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Scene Context — where/when this conversation happens and what 
       immediately precedes it.
    2. Characters and Voices — who's speaking, with reference to their 
       established distinct speech patterns (see Character Prompting).
    3. Individual Wants — what each character is trying to get out of 
       THIS specific conversation (these can conflict, creating 
       tension).
    4. Subtext Guidance — what's true beneath the surface that 
       characters aren't directly stating.
    5. Dramatic Purpose — what this dialogue needs to accomplish for 
       the larger narrative (reveal a secret, escalate conflict, show 
       a relationship shift).
    6. Naturalism Reminders — explicit instruction toward realistic 
       speech patterns (interruptions, incomplete sentences, avoiding 
       stated emotions) if needed.

Example skeleton:
    "Write a dialogue scene between [CHARACTER A] and [CHARACTER B]. 
    Context: [WHAT'S HAPPENING/PRECEDING THIS MOMENT]. 
    [CHARACTER A] wants: [GOAL FOR THIS CONVERSATION]. 
    [CHARACTER B] wants: [GOAL, IDEALLY IN SOME TENSION WITH A'S]. 
    Subtext: [WHAT'S TRUE BENEATH THE SURFACE THAT ISN'T STATED 
    DIRECTLY]. 
    This scene should accomplish: [DRAMATIC PURPOSE]. 
    Keep dialogue naturalistic — avoid characters directly stating 
    their emotions; reveal feeling through word choice, what's avoided, 
    and subtext instead."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Give each character in the scene a distinct, CONFLICTING or at least 
  DIFFERENT want for the conversation — dialogue between two characters 
  who want the same thing in the same way tends to lack tension.
- Explicitly request SUBTEXT — what's true but not directly stated — 
  since this is often what separates sophisticated dialogue from flat, 
  on-the-nose writing.
- Explicitly discourage characters STATING THEIR EMOTIONS directly 
  ("I'm really angry right now") in favor of revealing emotion through 
  word choice, what's avoided, interruptions, or behavior alongside 
  dialogue.
- Allow for NATURALISTIC IMPERFECTION — real speech includes 
  interruptions, trailing off, filler words, and grammatically 
  incomplete sentences; overly polished dialogue often reads as 
  unnatural.
- Reference each character's ESTABLISHED VOICE (from Character 
  Prompting work) explicitly so dialogue stays consistent with who 
  they are, not generic.
- State the scene's DRAMATIC PURPOSE clearly — dialogue should be doing 
  narrative work (revealing something, shifting a relationship, raising 
  stakes), not just filling space with pleasant or realistic-but-inert 
  chat.
- When REVISING flat or expository dialogue, specifically ask the 
  model to identify which lines are pure exposition and rework them to 
  emerge more naturally from character wants and conflict.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Both characters wanting the same thing from the conversation 
  with no real tension, resulting in flat, low-stakes dialogue.
  FIX: Give characters distinct, ideally conflicting wants for the 
  scene.

- MISTAKE: Characters directly stating their emotions ("I feel so 
  betrayed") rather than revealing feeling through word choice, 
  subtext, or behavior.
  FIX: Explicitly discourage direct emotional statements in favor of 
  implication.

- MISTAKE: Dialogue that reads as an information dump disguised as 
  conversation ("As you know, we've been working on this project for 
  three years...").
  FIX: Identify expository content and find ways to convey necessary 
  information through conflict/subtext rather than direct statement.

- MISTAKE: All characters sounding identical, with no distinguishing 
  vocabulary, sentence length, or verbal patterns.
  FIX: Explicitly reference each character's distinct established 
  voice.

- MISTAKE: Overly polished, grammatically perfect dialogue that doesn't 
  reflect how people actually speak (with interruptions, incomplete 
  thoughts, natural imperfection).
  FIX: Explicitly request naturalistic speech patterns including 
  appropriate imperfection.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a short dialogue scene between two friends, where one is trying 
to convince the other to go to a party they don't want to attend. 
Friend A wants: to not go alone. Friend B wants: to stay home without 
seeming antisocial. Keep it naturalistic — avoid either character 
directly stating how they feel; let it come through in what they say 
and avoid."

Expected Output:
A short, naturalistic exchange where Friend A's persistence and 
Friend B's deflections/excuses reveal their respective positions 
without either explicitly announcing "I don't want to go alone" or "I 
don't want to seem antisocial" — the underlying wants emerging through 
the pattern of the conversation itself.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a dialogue scene between a manager and an employee. Context: 
The employee has been underperforming, and the manager needs to address 
it, but the manager also privately knows the employee is going through 
a difficult personal situation (a fact the employee hasn't officially 
disclosed at work). 

Manager wants: to address the performance issue professionally without 
overstepping into personal territory the employee hasn't opened up 
about. 
Employee wants: to avoid admitting the personal struggle while also 
not seeming like they don't care about their work. 
Subtext: both people are aware something personal is going on, but 
neither directly names it. 

This scene should reveal the manager's genuine care beneath 
professional formality, and the employee's exhaustion/pride, without 
either being stated directly."

Expected Output:
A scene where the manager's careful, slightly-too-gentle phrasing of 
performance concerns hints at awareness beyond the professional 
surface, and the employee's slightly defensive, deflecting responses 
suggest exhaustion and pride without either party naming the actual 
personal situation — demonstrating dialogue carrying significant 
unstated meaning beneath a professionally appropriate surface 
conversation.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a dialogue scene between the two established characters from 
earlier development: MAYA (52, controlling but seeking genuine 
reconciliation) and DANIEL (48, resentful, withholds forgiveness even 
when offered) — siblings meeting for the first time in 15 years due to 
their mother's declining health. 

Context: This is their first private conversation since arriving, 
before they've discussed their mother's condition at all — the 
estrangement itself is the immediate, unavoidable elephant in the room.

Maya wants: to reestablish connection, but on her own terms — she'll 
apologize, but only in a way that also justifies her original decision 
to leave. 
Daniel wants: an apology that doesn't come with justification, but 
also, beneath his resentment, some flicker of genuine relief that 
she's here. 
Subtext: both actually want reconciliation, but their specific flaws 
(Maya's need for control/justification, Daniel's need to withhold 
forgiveness) are actively preventing them from simply saying so 
plainly. 

This scene should NOT resolve their conflict — it should show the 
attempt failing due to their established flaws, setting up further 
tension for later in the story. Maintain their distinct established 
voices: Maya more controlled/measured, Daniel more clipped and 
defensive."

Expected Output:
A dialogue scene where Maya's attempted apology becomes entangled with 
self-justification ("I know I hurt you, but you have to understand 
why I—"), triggering Daniel's defensive withholding response before 
she can even finish, with a brief, almost-visible crack in his 
resentment (a pause, a half-step toward softening) that he then 
suppresses — demonstrating both characters' established flaws actively 
sabotaging a reconciliation that some part of both of them genuinely 
wants, ending in continued impasse rather than resolution, consistent 
with the specified dramatic purpose of setting up further tension 
rather than resolving the conflict prematurely.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Write a dialogue scene between [CHARACTER A] and [CHARACTER B].
    
    Context: [WHERE/WHEN THIS HAPPENS, WHAT PRECEDES IT]
    
    [CHARACTER A]'s established voice: [SPEECH PATTERN NOTES]
    [CHARACTER A] wants (from this conversation): [SPECIFIC GOAL]
    
    [CHARACTER B]'s established voice: [SPEECH PATTERN NOTES]
    [CHARACTER B] wants (from this conversation): [SPECIFIC GOAL, 
    ideally in tension with A's]
    
    Subtext: [WHAT'S TRUE BENEATH THE SURFACE THAT ISN'T STATED 
    DIRECTLY]
    
    Dramatic purpose: [WHAT THIS SCENE NEEDS TO ACCOMPLISH — reveal, 
    escalate, shift a relationship]
    
    Keep dialogue naturalistic: avoid characters directly stating 
    their emotions; reveal feeling through word choice, subtext, and 
    what's left unsaid. Include natural speech imperfections 
    (interruptions, incomplete thoughts) where appropriate.
