46. STORY PROMPTING

Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 2: Creative)

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Story Prompting — Structuring Prompts for Narrative Fiction

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Story prompting is a technique specifically designed for generating 
NARRATIVE FICTION — short stories, longer fiction, plot outlines — by 
providing the model with the specific narrative elements a compelling 
story requires: genre, setting, characters, central conflict, tone, 
narrative arc/structure, and point of view. Unlike generic creative 
writing requests ("write a story about a dragon"), effective Story 
Prompting supplies the SCAFFOLDING that professional fiction relies on 
— giving the model enough narrative architecture to produce something 
with genuine dramatic shape rather than a flat, meandering sequence of 
events.

Think of it like the difference between telling someone "write a 
story" (leaving every narrative decision to chance) versus giving a 
screenwriter a proper story brief: genre, the protagonist's want and 
the obstacle in their way, the setting, the tone, and how it should 
resolve — the SAME creative freedom remains within that structure, but 
the structure itself dramatically improves the odds of a genuinely 
satisfying narrative.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Story prompting exists to:
- Provide the NARRATIVE SCAFFOLDING (conflict, arc, stakes) that 
  separates a genuinely compelling story from a flat sequence of 
  events with no dramatic shape.
- Give the model enough SPECIFIC creative constraints to produce 
  distinctive, non-generic fiction rather than defaulting to the most 
  cliché version of a genre.
- Support ITERATIVE STORY DEVELOPMENT — outlining first, then 
  expanding specific sections, rather than generating an entire story 
  in one unstructured pass.
- Enable CONSISTENT tone, voice, and stakes throughout a longer piece 
  by establishing these elements clearly upfront.
- Serve as the foundation that more specialized creative techniques 
  (Character Prompting, World-Building Prompting, Dialogue Prompting) 
  build upon for more focused narrative sub-tasks.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Writing short stories, flash fiction, or longer narrative fiction.
- Developing plot outlines or story structures before full drafting.
- Creative writing exercises, worldbuilding-adjacent fiction, or 
  narrative content for games/interactive media.
- Any task where the GOAL is genuine dramatic engagement (tension, 
  stakes, emotional arc), not just a description of events.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Purely informational or technical writing with no narrative element.
- Extremely short creative snippets (a single joke, a one-line 
  caption) where full narrative scaffolding is unnecessary overhead.
- When the person wants pure, unguided creative exploration without 
  any structural constraints (in this case, a very open prompt may be 
  more appropriate than heavy scaffolding).

BEST MODELS FOR STORY PROMPTING:
- Claude — widely regarded as excellent for nuanced, emotionally 
  resonant, well-paced fiction with a strong sense of voice and 
  restraint from overly purple prose.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong narrative capability, versatile across 
  genres and tones.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra — capable storytelling, particularly strong when 
  combined with multimodal elements (illustrated stories, 
  screenplay-to-visual pipelines).
- Grok — capable of more irreverent, unconventional narrative styles 
  given its design philosophy.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of genre fiction, especially larger 
  variants; may lean more heavily on genre tropes/clichés without 
  strong, specific prompting to push toward more distinctive choices.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — functional for narrative tasks but generally less 
  specialized/refined for creative fiction compared to models with 
  more literary-focused training emphasis.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [GENRE] + [SETTING] + [PROTAGONIST + WANT] + [CENTRAL CONFLICT/
    OBSTACLE] + [TONE] + [LENGTH] + [POV] + [DESIRED RESOLUTION TYPE]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Genre/Tone — the overall style and emotional register (dark 
       comedy, literary fiction, hard sci-fi, cozy mystery).
    2. Setting — where and when the story takes place.
    3. Protagonist and Want — who the story follows and what they 
       need/desire.
    4. Central Conflict/Obstacle — what stands between the protagonist 
       and their want, creating the story's tension.
    5. Point of View — first person, third person limited, omniscient, 
       etc.
    6. Length/Format — short story, flash fiction (under 1000 words), 
       chapter, outline only.
    7. Resolution Guidance (optional) — hint at desired ending type 
       (bittersweet, twist, open-ended) without necessarily dictating 
       every plot beat.

Example skeleton:
    "Write a [GENRE] [FORMAT/LENGTH] set in [SETTING]. 
    Protagonist: [NAME/DESCRIPTION], who wants [GOAL]. 
    Central conflict: [OBSTACLE/ANTAGONIST/TENSION]. 
    Tone: [TONE DESCRIPTION]. 
    POV: [PERSPECTIVE]. 
    [OPTIONAL: resolution guidance]"

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Give the protagonist a SPECIFIC, CONCRETE want, not just a vague 
  trait — "wants to reconcile with her estranged father before he 
  dies" drives a story; "is complicated" does not.
- Specify the CENTRAL CONFLICT explicitly — a story needs genuine 
  friction/stakes to have dramatic shape; without this, even good 
  prose reads as a flat sequence of events.
- Provide enough SPECIFICITY in setting/genre to avoid the model 
  defaulting to the most generic, cliché version of that genre (e.g., 
  "cyberpunk story" vs. "cyberpunk story set in a flooded, half-
  abandoned coastal megacity where analog technology has become a 
  status symbol among the ultra-rich").
- For longer pieces, consider requesting an OUTLINE first (major plot 
  beats), reviewing it, then requesting full prose expansion — this 
  mirrors Plan-and-Solve Prompting (file 24) applied to fiction.
- State the desired TONE explicitly and precisely — "darkly comic," 
  "quietly devastating," "wry and understated" each produce very 
  different prose even for an identical plot.
- Avoid over-specifying EVERY plot beat if you want genuine creative 
  surprise — provide the scaffolding (conflict, stakes, tone) and 
  leave room for the model's own narrative choices within that frame.
- For genre fiction specifically, consider naming what CLICHÉS to 
  avoid, which often produces more distinctive results than positive 
  instructions alone.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Vague, trait-only protagonist description with no concrete 
  want, producing a story with no clear dramatic engine.
  FIX: Give the protagonist a specific, concrete goal or need driving 
  the narrative.

- MISTAKE: No explicit central conflict/obstacle, resulting in a flat 
  sequence of events rather than a story with genuine tension.
  FIX: Always specify what stands in the protagonist's way.

- MISTAKE: Overly generic genre/setting specification, leading to the 
  most cliché possible version of that genre.
  FIX: Add specific, distinctive details to setting and premise.

- MISTAKE: Attempting a full, long narrative in one single generation 
  pass without any outlining step, risking pacing and structural 
  issues in longer pieces.
  FIX: For longer fiction, outline first, then expand section by 
  section.

- MISTAKE: Over-specifying every single plot beat, leaving no room for 
  the model's own creative narrative choices within the given 
  scaffolding.
  FIX: Provide the essential structural elements (conflict, stakes, 
  tone) while leaving specific narrative execution open.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a short flash fiction story (under 500 words). Genre: quiet, 
literary. Setting: a small coastal town in autumn. Protagonist: an 
elderly fisherman who wants to pass down a family recipe before his 
memory fades further. Central conflict: he keeps forgetting a key 
ingredient each time he tries to write it down. Tone: gently 
melancholic but ultimately warm. POV: third person limited."

Expected Output:
A complete flash fiction piece under 500 words with genuine narrative 
shape — the fisherman's repeated, frustrated attempts to record the 
recipe, building gentle melancholy, likely resolving with some form of 
warmth or acceptance (perhaps someone else stepping in to help, or 
finding peace with imperfect memory) — written in close third person 
with restrained, literary prose matching the specified tone.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a short sci-fi story outline (bullet-point plot beats, not full 
prose yet) for a story with this premise: 

Genre: hard sci-fi with a personal, intimate focus (not epic space 
opera). 
Setting: a solitary research station on Europa, near-future. 
Protagonist: Dr. Amara Chen, a xenobiologist who has just detected a 
signal that could be evidence of alien life — but confirming it 
requires her to sabotage the station's safety protocols in a way that 
endangers her own crew. 
Central conflict: scientific ambition and the desire for a 
world-changing discovery vs. genuine risk to people she cares about. 
Tone: tense, morally ambiguous, restrained (not melodramatic). 
POV: first person. 

Provide 6-8 major plot beats as an outline, without writing full 
prose."

Expected Output:
A structured outline of 6-8 plot beats tracing Amara's escalating 
internal conflict — likely including the initial signal detection, an 
early smaller risk she takes and its cost, escalating pressure/
temptation, a genuine moment of crew endangerment, a moral reckoning, 
and an ending that doesn't resolve too neatly (matching the specified 
"morally ambiguous, restrained" tone) — providing scaffolding for 
later full-prose expansion rather than complete narrative text.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"I want to write a literary short story exploring themes of memory 
and inherited trauma, avoiding common clichés in this space (avoid: a 
literal magical artifact representing memory, an overly neat 
therapy-session revelation scene, a dead relative's ghost literally 
appearing). 

Genre: literary fiction with subtle magical realism. 
Setting: contemporary Kolkata, during Durga Puja. 
Protagonist: Ronit, a young man visiting his grandmother's ancestral 
home for the first time since her death, who begins noticing that 
certain rooms in the house seem to shift subtly each time he 
re-enters them — not fantastical or explained, just quietly, 
unsettlingly different. 
Central conflict: Ronit's need to understand his grandmother 
(who he barely knew) and a family silence around her own mother's 
disappearance decades earlier, mirrored by the house's own instability. 
Tone: quiet, atmospheric, unresolved in a way that feels intentional 
rather than incomplete. 
POV: close third person. 
Length: 1200-1500 words. 

First provide a brief outline (4-5 beats), then, if the outline looks 
right, I'll ask you to expand it into full prose."

Expected Output:
A thoughtful outline avoiding all three specified clichés, using the 
shifting-rooms motif as a genuinely subtle, ambiguous magical-realist 
device (rather than an explained supernatural mechanism) that 
externalizes the family's unspoken history, tracing Ronit's growing 
unease and partial, incomplete understanding — building toward an 
ending that deliberately resists tidy resolution, appropriate to the 
specified tone — demonstrating sophisticated genre awareness and 
active avoidance of the named clichés rather than falling back on them 
by default.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Write a [GENRE/TONE] [FORMAT: short story/flash fiction/outline] 
    of approximately [LENGTH].
    
    Setting: [WHERE AND WHEN]
    
    Protagonist: [NAME/DESCRIPTION], who wants [SPECIFIC GOAL/NEED]
    
    Central conflict: [WHAT STANDS IN THEIR WAY / THE SOURCE OF 
    TENSION]
    
    Tone: [SPECIFIC TONE DESCRIPTORS]
    
    POV: [FIRST PERSON / THIRD PERSON LIMITED / OMNISCIENT]
    
    [OPTIONAL: Avoid these clichés: [LIST]]
    [OPTIONAL: Resolution guidance: [e.g., bittersweet, open-ended, 
    twist]]
