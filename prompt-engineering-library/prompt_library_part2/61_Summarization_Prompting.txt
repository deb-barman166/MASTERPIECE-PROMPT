-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Summarization Prompting — Condensing Content While Preserving Meaning

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Summarization prompting is a technique for instructing an LLM to 
condense longer content into a SHORTER form that preserves the most 
important information, while explicitly controlling the summary's 
LENGTH, FOCUS (what to prioritize), and STYLE (bullet points vs. 
prose, executive summary vs. detailed abstract). Effective 
summarization requires more specification than "make this shorter" — 
different use cases need dramatically different summary types (a one-
line takeaway vs. a detailed abstract vs. a bulleted key-points list), 
and without guidance the model must guess at your actual needs.

Think of it like the difference between asking someone to "tell me 
about this book" and getting a wildly variable response depending on 
their guess about what you want, versus specifically requesting "give 
me the 3 main plot points in one sentence each, suitable for someone 
deciding whether to read it" — the SAME source material, but 
dramatically different, purpose-fit outputs depending on exactly what 
kind of summary is actually useful for your situation.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Summarization prompting exists to:
- Save TIME by extracting the essential content from lengthy material 
  without requiring the reader to process the full original.
- Support different DOWNSTREAM USES of the same source material — an 
  executive needs different summary depth/focus than someone doing 
  detailed technical follow-up.
- Preserve ACCURACY while reducing length — a good summary doesn't just 
  shorten text, it correctly identifies and retains what's genuinely 
  most important while cutting what's genuinely less essential.
- Enable QUICK TRIAGE across large volumes of content (many documents, 
  long meeting transcripts, extensive reports) to identify what deserves 
  deeper attention.
- Respect copyright and fair-use principles by producing genuinely 
  transformed, condensed content rather than a lightly-reworded near-
  copy of the original (directly connecting to proper summarization 
  practice as distinct from disguised reproduction).

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Condensing long documents, articles, reports, transcripts, or 
  research papers into a more digestible form.
- Creating executive summaries, abstracts, or key-takeaway lists for 
  different audiences.
- Triaging large volumes of content to identify what deserves deeper 
  attention.
- Extracting the essential content from meeting notes, long email 
  threads, or extended discussions.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Content that's already appropriately concise — summarizing a 
  paragraph into a sentence rarely adds value.
- When you actually need the FULL detail and a summary would cause you 
  to miss important nuance for your specific purpose.
- Creative writing content where "summarizing" would strip away exactly 
  what makes it valuable (voice, specific imagery, emotional 
  experience) — plot summaries of fiction serve a different purpose 
  than the fiction itself.

BEST MODELS FOR SUMMARIZATION PROMPTING:
- Claude (especially with large context windows) — excellent at 
  accurate, well-calibrated summarization that correctly identifies 
  genuinely important content rather than just extracting the first/
  last parts of a document.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong summarization capability across many 
  content types and requested formats.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra (especially with 1M-2M token context) — exceptional 
  for summarizing extremely long documents, entire books, or large 
  document collections in a single pass.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — capable general summarization, particularly 
  reliable for technical/structured content.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of summarization tasks; for very long 
  source material approaching context limits, verify the model can 
  actually process the full length without truncation issues.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [SOURCE CONTENT] + "Summarize this in [LENGTH/FORMAT], focusing on 
    [SPECIFIC PRIORITY]. Audience: [WHO WILL READ THIS SUMMARY]."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Source Content — the material to be summarized.
    2. Length Specification — a specific target (one sentence, 3 
       bullet points, 200 words, a single paragraph) rather than a 
       vague "shorter."
    3. Format — prose paragraph, bulleted list, executive summary 
       structure, key-takeaways format.
    4. Focus/Priority — what specifically matters most for this summary 
       (financial implications, action items, main arguments, 
       technical findings) since different priorities lead to genuinely 
       different valid summaries of the same source.
    5. Audience — who will read the summary, affecting appropriate 
       vocabulary and depth.

Example skeleton:
    "Summarize the following [CONTENT TYPE] in [SPECIFIC LENGTH/
    FORMAT]. 
    
    Focus specifically on: [PRIORITY — e.g., action items, financial 
    implications, main arguments]. 
    
    Audience: [WHO WILL READ THIS]. 
    
    Content: 
    [SOURCE CONTENT]"

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Specify an EXACT LENGTH TARGET (word count, sentence count, bullet 
  point count) rather than a vague "make it shorter" — this removes 
  ambiguity about how aggressively to condense.
- Define the FOCUS/PRIORITY explicitly — the same source document 
  summarized for "financial implications" vs. "technical risks" vs. 
  "action items" should genuinely produce different, appropriately 
  focused summaries, not the same generic overview each time.
- Specify the AUDIENCE so vocabulary and depth are appropriately 
  calibrated (executives typically want different depth than technical 
  peers reviewing the same material).
- Request a specific FORMAT (bullets vs. prose, structured sections vs. 
  single paragraph) matching how the summary will actually be used or 
  read.
- For genuinely important summaries, ask the model to flag anything 
  significant it had to OMIT due to length constraints, so you're aware 
  of what's being left out.
- Remember copyright/fair-use principles when summarizing copyrighted 
  material (per Citation Prompting and copyright-compliance practices) 
  — genuine summarization means substantial transformation and 
  condensation, not a lightly-reworded near-copy of the original text.
- For VERY long source material, verify it actually fits within the 
  model's context window; for content exceeding this, consider chunked 
  summarization (summarize sections, then summarize the summaries) or 
  RAG-based approaches.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Requesting a vague "shorter version" without specifying an 
  actual length target, leading to inconsistent or inappropriately-
  sized results.
  FIX: Always specify a concrete length target.

- MISTAKE: Not specifying focus/priority, resulting in a generic 
  overview that may not emphasize what actually matters for your 
  specific purpose.
  FIX: Explicitly state what the summary should prioritize.

- MISTAKE: Not specifying the target audience, leading to inappropriate 
  vocabulary/depth calibration.
  FIX: Explicitly define who will read the summary.

- MISTAKE: Treating a summary as capturing everything important without 
  asking what had to be omitted, potentially missing awareness of 
  significant excluded content.
  FIX: For important summaries, ask what significant content was 
  necessarily left out.

- MISTAKE: Producing a summary that's really just a lightly reworded, 
  still-substantial near-copy of the original rather than genuine 
  condensation — a copyright and quality concern.
  FIX: Request genuine synthesis/condensation, not paraphrased 
  reproduction of similar length.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Summarize this article in exactly 3 bullet points, focusing on the 
main findings. Audience: someone deciding whether to read the full 
article. 

Article: [pasted article text]"

Expected Output:
Exactly 3 bullet points capturing the article's main findings, concise 
enough to help a reader quickly assess relevance without reading the 
full piece.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Summarize this 45-minute meeting transcript in a structured executive 
summary format (under 250 words total), with these sections: Key 
Decisions Made, Action Items (with owners if mentioned), and Open 
Questions. Focus specifically on decisions and next steps, not the 
full discussion detail. Audience: team members who couldn't attend the 
meeting and need to know what they missed for their own work. 

Transcript: [pasted transcript]"

Expected Output:
A structured, under-250-word executive summary with the three specified 
sections, correctly extracting actual decisions made (not just topics 
discussed), action items with owners where the transcript specifies 
them, and genuinely open/unresolved questions — appropriately focused 
on what absent team members actually need to know for their own 
follow-up work.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Summarize this technical research paper on retrieval-augmented 
generation techniques in two DIFFERENT formats for two different 
audiences: 

Format 1: A 2-sentence summary suitable for a non-technical stakeholder 
deciding whether this research is relevant to our product roadmap — 
focus purely on practical implications, avoid technical jargon entirely.

Format 2: A 200-word technical abstract suitable for a fellow ML 
engineer, focusing on the specific methodology, key results/metrics, 
and how this compares to standard RAG approaches — technical vocabulary 
is appropriate and expected here.

Flag anything significant that had to be omitted from Format 1 due to 
its extreme brevity, so I'm aware of what's being simplified away for 
that audience. 

Paper: [pasted paper text]"

Expected Output:
Two genuinely distinct summaries appropriately calibrated to their 
stated audiences: Format 1 in plain, jargon-free language focusing 
purely on practical product relevance in just 2 sentences; Format 2 
using appropriate technical vocabulary, specific methodology details, 
and quantitative results suitable for an ML engineer peer — followed by 
an explicit note on what technical nuance/methodology detail was 
necessarily sacrificed in the ultra-brief Format 1 version, giving the 
requester awareness of that trade-off.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Summarize the following [CONTENT TYPE] in [SPECIFIC LENGTH: e.g., 
    3 bullet points / 200 words / one paragraph], in [FORMAT: prose/
    bullets/structured sections].
    
    Focus specifically on: [PRIORITY — e.g., action items, financial 
    implications, main arguments, technical findings]
    
    Audience: [WHO WILL READ THIS SUMMARY AND THEIR BACKGROUND]
    
    [OPTIONAL: Flag anything significant that had to be omitted due to 
    length constraints.]
    
    Content:
    [PASTE SOURCE CONTENT]
