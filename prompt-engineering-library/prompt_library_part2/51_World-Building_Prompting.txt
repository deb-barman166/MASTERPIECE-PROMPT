-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
World-Building Prompting — Constructing Coherent Fictional Settings

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
World-Building prompting is a technique for developing the complete, 
internally CONSISTENT fictional SETTING a narrative takes place in — 
its history, geography, rules (magic systems, technology levels, 
physics), cultures, politics, economics, and social structures — with 
enough depth and internal logic that the world feels lived-in and 
coherent rather than an arbitrary, inconsistent backdrop assembled 
purely to serve immediate plot needs. This is distinct from Setting 
description within Story Prompting (file 46), which covers just the 
immediate backdrop of ONE story; World-Building is the broader, often 
reusable foundation that MULTIPLE stories, characters, or an entire 
franchise can be built upon.

Think of it like the difference between describing the single room a 
scene takes place in (Setting, part of Story Prompting) versus 
designing an entire country's history, government, economy, and 
culture that could support dozens of different stories set within it 
— the difference between a stage set and an actual functioning, 
internally logical civilization.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
World-Building prompting exists to:
- Create INTERNALLY CONSISTENT fictional settings where the rules 
  (magic, technology, politics, economics) remain coherent and don't 
  contradict themselves across a larger narrative project.
- Provide a rich, reusable FOUNDATION that can support multiple 
  stories, characters, or an entire franchise, rather than a one-off 
  backdrop.
- Generate the kind of texture and depth (unique customs, believable 
  economic systems, plausible political tensions) that makes fictional 
  worlds feel genuinely lived-in rather than generic.
- Establish CONSTRAINTS that actually generate interesting story 
  possibilities — a well-designed magic system's LIMITATIONS often 
  create more compelling conflict than its raw capabilities.
- Support collaborative or long-term creative projects (novel series, 
  game worlds, tabletop RPG settings) where a shared, documented world 
  reference prevents inconsistency over time.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Developing settings for fantasy, science fiction, or any genre 
  requiring invented geography, history, politics, or fictional 
  systems (magic, advanced technology).
- Building a foundation intended to support MULTIPLE stories or a 
  long-running series/franchise.
- Game world design (video games, tabletop RPGs) requiring rich, 
  explorable settings.
- Any project where world consistency needs to be tracked and 
  referenced across a long creative process.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Contemporary, real-world-set fiction requiring no invented setting 
  elements.
- Very short pieces where an immediate scene description (Story 
  Prompting's setting element) is sufficient without needing a full 
  world-building foundation.
- When the creative focus is purely character/plot-driven and 
  extensive setting development would be disproportionate effort.

BEST MODELS FOR WORLD-BUILDING PROMPTING:
- Claude — strong at maintaining logical consistency across complex, 
  multi-system world details (tracking how a magic system's rules 
  interact with the political structure it enables, for example).
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong world-building capability, versatile 
  across genre conventions and good at generating rich supporting 
  detail.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra — capable world-building, particularly useful when 
  paired with visual concept generation for maps, creature/culture 
  design.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — functional but generally less specialized for 
  richly textured creative world-building compared to models with more 
  literary/creative training emphasis.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of world-building tasks, especially larger 
  variants; for very complex, multi-system worlds, may benefit from 
  building the world INCREMENTALLY (one system at a time) rather than 
  attempting full complexity in one generation.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [GENRE/SETTING TYPE] + [CORE PREMISE/UNIQUE HOOK] + [HISTORY] + 
    [GEOGRAPHY] + [RULES/SYSTEMS - magic/tech/physics] + [CULTURES/
    POLITICS] + [ECONOMY] + [CURRENT TENSIONS/CONFLICTS]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Genre and Core Premise — the overall type of world and its 
       unique defining hook (what makes this world distinct, not 
       generic).
    2. History — key past events that shaped the current state of the 
       world (only what's relevant to present-day stories, not an 
       exhaustive timeline).
    3. Geography — the physical layout relevant to the stories that 
       will be told here.
    4. Rules/Systems — how magic, technology, or altered physics work, 
       INCLUDING their limitations (limitations create story 
       potential).
    5. Cultures and Politics — the social/governmental structures and 
       how different groups relate to and conflict with each other.
    6. Economy — how resources, wealth, and trade function, since 
       economic systems often drive political tension.
    7. Current Tensions — the present-day conflicts/pressures that 
       could drive multiple different stories.

Example skeleton:
    "World: [GENRE/SETTING TYPE] with the core premise: [UNIQUE HOOK]. 
    History: [KEY PAST EVENTS]. 
    Geography: [RELEVANT PHYSICAL LAYOUT]. 
    Rules/Systems: [HOW MAGIC/TECH/PHYSICS WORK, INCLUDING 
    LIMITATIONS]. 
    Cultures/Politics: [SOCIAL AND GOVERNMENTAL STRUCTURE]. 
    Economy: [HOW RESOURCES/TRADE FUNCTION]. 
    Current tensions: [PRESENT-DAY CONFLICTS/PRESSURES]."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Define LIMITATIONS as carefully as capabilities for any invented 
  system (magic, technology) — a magic system with no real limits 
  removes narrative tension, since a limitless-power character can 
  simply solve any problem; interesting constraints generate interesting 
  stories.
- Ground world elements in genuine INTERNAL LOGIC — if magic requires 
  a physical cost, that cost should have consistent, traceable 
  consequences throughout the world (economic effects, social 
  stigma/status, political implications).
- Focus depth on what's actually RELEVANT to the stories you intend to 
  tell — an exhaustively detailed economic system for a story that 
  never touches economics wastes effort; prioritize the systems your 
  narrative will actually engage with.
- Build in CURRENT TENSIONS/conflicts rather than a static, peaceful 
  world — active political, social, or resource tensions are what 
  generate story possibilities.
- For complex worlds, consider building INCREMENTALLY — establish core 
  premise and rules first, then develop cultures/politics, then 
  economics, reviewing consistency at each stage rather than attempting 
  everything simultaneously.
- Maintain a WORLD BIBLE/reference document for ongoing projects, and 
  re-provide key established facts when writing new content set in 
  this world to prevent contradictions accumulating over time.
- Avoid GENERIC genre defaults — actively look for what makes THIS 
  world's magic system, political structure, or culture distinct from 
  the most common version of that genre trope.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Designing a magic/technology system with no real 
  limitations, removing narrative tension since characters can simply 
  solve problems with unconstrained power.
  FIX: Always define specific, consistent limitations alongside 
  capabilities.

- MISTAKE: Building extensive detail in areas the actual stories won't 
  engage with, while under-developing systems central to the intended 
  narrative.
  FIX: Prioritize depth in the systems most relevant to your actual 
  story plans.

- MISTAKE: Creating a static world with no active tensions or 
  conflicts, missing the generative potential that political/social/
  resource pressures provide for story ideas.
  FIX: Explicitly establish current tensions that could drive multiple 
  different narratives.

- MISTAKE: Inconsistency accumulating across a long project because 
  established facts aren't tracked or re-referenced in later writing 
  sessions.
  FIX: Maintain a world reference document and re-provide key facts 
  when generating new content.

- MISTAKE: Defaulting to the most generic, cliché version of genre 
  conventions (standard medieval-Europe fantasy, standard dystopian 
  megacity) without adding distinctive elements.
  FIX: Actively identify and incorporate what makes this specific 
  world distinct from genre defaults.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Develop a basic fantasy world concept. Core premise: magic is powered 
by memories — using a spell means permanently losing a specific 
memory, with more powerful spells costing more significant memories. 
This creates a society where powerful mages are often somewhat 
'hollowed out' people who've traded away much of their personal 
history. Briefly describe how this affects who chooses to become a 
mage and how society views them."

Expected Output:
A world concept exploring how the memory-cost magic system would 
create specific social dynamics — perhaps mages being both respected 
for their power and pitied/feared for what they've sacrificed, 
attracting people with few memories worth keeping (the desperate, the 
already-traumatized) alongside genuine idealists willing to make the 
trade for a greater cause — demonstrating how a well-defined limitation 
generates rich social/character implications beyond just the magic 
mechanic itself.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Develop a science fiction world: a generation ship, 200 years into a 
300-year journey to a new habitable planet, where the original crew's 
descendants have developed their own distinct culture. 

History: Briefly note what's changed culturally/socially in 200 years 
of isolation. 
Geography: The ship's internal layout — key sections/decks relevant to 
social stratification. 
Rules/Systems: How resources (air, water, food, power) are managed and 
rationed, and what happens to those who violate resource rules. 
Politics: How the ship is governed now, and how this differs from its 
original founding charter. 
Current tensions: What conflict is brewing as the ship approaches its 
final 100 years, given generational drift from the original mission's 
purpose."

Expected Output:
A coherent generation-ship setting where 200 years of isolation has 
plausibly created a distinct culture/dialect, a resource-management 
system that has calcified into strict social stratification (those 
managing critical systems holding power over resource access), a 
political structure that has drifted from its original charter 
(perhaps a founding democracy has become a technocracy or hereditary 
system), and a current tension around growing skepticism among younger 
generations about whether the original mission (to a planet none of 
them have seen and can barely imagine) is still worth the sacrifices 
being demanded of the present generation — providing rich material for 
multiple possible stories.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Develop a complex fantasy political world avoiding standard medieval-
Europe defaults. Core premise: a coastal trading confederation of 
semi-independent city-states, where political power is tied not to 
land ownership or bloodline, but to control of a rare, magically-
reactive mineral used to power both defensive wards AND long-distance 
communication networks — making its distribution simultaneously a 
military, economic, and information-control issue. 

History: How this mineral's discovery 150 years ago reshaped what was 
previously a more traditional feudal structure into the current 
confederation system. 
Geography: Where the mineral is actually found (creating a specific, 
contested region) versus where the wealthy trading hubs that process/
distribute it are located (creating a producer/processor economic 
tension). 
Rules/Systems: How the mineral's wards and communication functions 
actually work, and critically, its LIMITATIONS (what it can't do, what 
degrades its function, what makes it scarce/hard to extract safely). 
Cultures/Politics: How the confederation's member city-states relate 
to each other, and how the mineral-producing region (likely poorer, 
more exploited) relates politically to the wealthier processing/
trading hubs. 
Economy: The specific economic relationship between extraction, 
processing, and the wealth this generates for different parties. 
Current tensions: What's currently straining the confederation, given 
the described producer/processor economic imbalance."

Expected Output:
A richly logical world where the "magic mineral" isn't just a generic 
fantasy resource but is woven into political structure (power = 
control of ward/communication tech), economic tension (a plausible 
resource-curse dynamic where the extraction region is poorer than the 
processing/trading hubs despite being the actual source of value), and 
specific limitations (perhaps the mineral degrades over time requiring 
constant fresh supply, or becomes unstable past a certain 
concentration, creating genuine technical/safety constraints rather 
than limitless magic) — culminating in a current tension that follows 
logically from the established economic imbalance (perhaps the 
extraction region's city-states are pushing for a larger share of 
trading profits or greater political representation, threatening the 
confederation's stability) — demonstrating deep internal consistency 
across history, geography, systems, politics, and economics all 
connecting back to the single core premise rather than feeling like 
disconnected, arbitrary worldbuilding elements.
