-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Evaluation Prompting — Scoring Content Against Defined Quality Criteria

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Evaluation prompting is a technique for instructing an LLM to assess 
the QUALITY of content — an essay, a piece of code, a business plan, 
another AI's output — against explicit, defined criteria, typically 
producing a SCORE or RATING alongside justification. This is closely 
related to but broader than Critic Prompting (file 29, a specific 
multi-agent architectural role) — Evaluation Prompting is the general 
technique of using an LLM AS A JUDGE across any domain, including 
grading, benchmarking, and increasingly, "LLM-as-judge" systems used to 
automatically evaluate the outputs of OTHER AI systems at scale.

Think of it like the difference between a teacher grading an essay 
against a defined rubric (Evaluation Prompting generally) versus a 
specific quality-assurance step in a software pipeline where one AI's 
output is fed to another AI specifically tasked with judging it before 
release (a common LLM-as-judge application) — both are the same 
underlying technique of scoring content against explicit standards, 
just applied in different contexts.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Evaluation prompting exists to:
- Provide CONSISTENT, RUBRIC-BASED assessment of content quality, 
  reducing the subjectivity/variability of ad-hoc judgment.
- Support AUTOMATED QUALITY GATES in content generation or AI 
  pipelines, where outputs need to be scored before being accepted, 
  published, or passed to a next stage (this is the core "LLM-as-judge" 
  pattern increasingly used to evaluate AI system outputs at scale).
- Enable BENCHMARKING and comparison of multiple outputs/options against 
  the same standardized criteria.
- Support EDUCATIONAL grading/feedback contexts where consistent 
  application of a rubric matters for fairness.
- Provide scalable evaluation for situations where human review of 
  every single output isn't practical (either due to volume or the need 
  for rapid iteration).

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Grading or scoring written work, code, or creative content against 
  defined criteria.
- Building automated quality gates for AI-generated content pipelines 
  (LLM-as-judge patterns).
- Benchmarking or comparing multiple pieces of content/multiple AI 
  system outputs against standardized criteria.
- Any situation requiring consistent, rubric-based assessment at scale.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Purely subjective creative feedback where a numeric score would feel 
  reductive or miss the point (though qualitative Evaluation Prompting 
  without a numeric score remains valuable here).
- High-stakes evaluations (like actual academic grading with real 
  consequences, or safety-critical system evaluation) where AI 
  evaluation should supplement, not fully replace, human judgment.
- Situations where the evaluation criteria themselves are genuinely 
  unclear or contested — establish clear criteria first before 
  attempting evaluation.

BEST MODELS FOR EVALUATION PROMPTING:
- Claude — well-regarded for thoughtful, well-calibrated evaluation 
  with genuine engagement with rubric criteria rather than superficial 
  scoring, commonly used in LLM-as-judge research and production 
  systems.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — strong evaluation capability, widely used in 
  both academic LLM-as-judge research and production quality-gate 
  systems.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra — capable evaluation, particularly strong for 
  technical/code evaluation tasks.
- DeepSeek / Qwen — capable evaluation performance, cost-effective for 
  high-volume automated evaluation pipelines.
- IMPORTANT PRACTICAL NOTE: For genuinely important evaluation use 
  cases, using a DIFFERENT (often more capable) model as the "judge" 
  than the model that GENERATED the content being evaluated tends to 
  reduce potential bias/blind-spot overlap between generator and judge.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [CONTENT TO EVALUATE] + "Evaluate against these criteria: 
    [RUBRIC]. Score each criterion [SCALE] with justification. Provide 
    an overall score."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Content to Evaluate — the specific output/work being assessed.
    2. Evaluation Rubric — explicit, defined criteria with clear 
       descriptions of what different score levels mean (avoid vague 
       criteria like "quality" without specifying what that means 
       concretely).
    3. Scoring Scale — the specific scale to use (1-10, 1-5, Pass/Fail, 
       letter grades) applied consistently across criteria.
    4. Justification Requirement — explicit request for reasoning 
       behind each score, not just a bare number.
    5. Overall Score/Verdict — how individual criterion scores combine 
       into an overall assessment (weighted average, minimum threshold, 
       holistic judgment).

Example skeleton:
    "Evaluate the following [CONTENT TYPE] against this rubric: 
    
    - [CRITERION 1] (1-10): [what different score levels mean] 
    - [CRITERION 2] (1-10): [what different score levels mean] 
    - [CRITERION 3] (1-10): [what different score levels mean] 
    
    For each criterion, provide a score and brief justification. Then 
    provide an overall score/verdict. 
    
    Content: [CONTENT TO EVALUATE]"

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Define EXPLICIT, CONCRETE criteria rather than vague ones — "clarity" 
  is vague; "can a reader unfamiliar with the topic understand the main 
  point within the first two sentences" is concrete and actionable for 
  consistent scoring.
- Describe what DIFFERENT SCORE LEVELS actually mean for each criterion 
  (what distinguishes a 3 from a 7 on a 10-point scale) rather than 
  just providing a bare numeric range, which improves scoring 
  consistency.
- ALWAYS require JUSTIFICATION alongside scores — a bare number provides 
  little insight or trust; reasoning lets you verify the evaluation 
  makes sense and understand specifically what drove the score.
- For LLM-as-judge production systems, consider using a model DIFFERENT 
  FROM the generator to reduce potential shared blind spots or biases.
- Test your evaluation rubric/prompt against content with KNOWN quality 
  levels (something you'd confidently call excellent, and something 
  you'd confidently call poor) to verify the scoring behaves sensibly 
  before trusting it for genuinely uncertain cases.
- For HIGH-STAKES evaluation (real grades, safety-critical assessments), 
  treat AI evaluation as a valuable input/first-pass, not a full 
  replacement for human judgment.
- Consider requesting evaluation from MULTIPLE ANGLES/perspectives for 
  especially important assessments (similar to the multiple-critique-
  pass practice in Self-Critique Prompting), rather than relying on a 
  single evaluation pass for consequential decisions.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Vague, undefined criteria ("rate the quality 1-10") that 
  don't specify what actually distinguishes different score levels, 
  leading to inconsistent or unclear scoring.
  FIX: Define concrete, specific criteria with clear descriptions of 
  what different score levels represent.

- MISTAKE: Requesting only a bare numeric score without justification, 
  providing little insight into what actually drove the evaluation.
  FIX: Always require justification/reasoning alongside every score.

- MISTAKE: Using the same model to both generate AND evaluate content 
  in high-stakes production systems, risking shared blind spots.
  FIX: Consider using a different model as evaluator for important use 
  cases.

- MISTAKE: Never testing the evaluation prompt/rubric against content 
  with known quality levels before trusting it for genuinely uncertain 
  cases.
  FIX: Validate your evaluation approach against clear-cut examples 
  first.

- MISTAKE: Treating AI evaluation as a complete replacement for human 
  judgment in genuinely high-stakes contexts (real academic grades, 
  safety assessments).
  FIX: Use AI evaluation as a valuable complement/first-pass for high-
  stakes decisions, not a full replacement.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Evaluate this product tagline against this rubric: 

- Clarity (1-5): 5 = immediately understandable, 1 = confusing 
- Memorability (1-5): 5 = distinctive and sticky, 1 = generic and 
forgettable 
- Brand fit (1-5): 5 = matches an eco-friendly, premium brand voice, 
1 = mismatched tone 

Score each with brief justification, then give an overall score. 

Tagline: 'Sustainability, redefined for the modern home.'"

Expected Output:
Individual scores for Clarity, Memorability, and Brand fit (e.g., 
Clarity: 4, reasonably clear though "redefined" is slightly abstract; 
Memorability: 3, competent but not especially distinctive; Brand fit: 
5, strong match for an eco-friendly premium positioning) with brief 
justification for each, followed by an overall score/summary.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Evaluate this Python function submission for a coding assessment 
against this rubric: 

- Correctness (1-10): 10 = handles all cases correctly including 
edge cases, 5 = handles basic cases but misses some edge cases, 1 = 
fundamentally incorrect 
- Code quality (1-10): 10 = clean, well-named, appropriately 
documented, 5 = functional but has readability issues, 1 = very hard 
to read/maintain 
- Efficiency (1-10): 10 = optimal time/space complexity for the 
problem, 5 = works but has clear inefficiencies, 1 = severely 
inefficient 

For each criterion, score and justify. Then provide an overall score 
(average) and a one-sentence summary verdict. 

Submission: [pasted function solving a 'find duplicates in array' 
problem using a nested-loop O(n²) approach with reasonable variable 
naming but no docstring]"

Expected Output:
Individual scores with justification (e.g., Correctness: 9, likely 
correctly handles the core cases though worth checking edge cases 
like empty arrays; Code quality: 6, reasonable naming but missing 
documentation; Efficiency: 4, the O(n²) nested-loop approach is 
notably inefficient compared to an O(n) hash-set-based solution), an 
overall average score, and a concise one-sentence verdict summarizing 
the submission's main strength and primary area for improvement.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"You are functioning as an automated LLM-as-judge quality gate for an 
AI content generation pipeline. Evaluate this AI-generated customer 
support response against this rubric before it's approved for 
sending: 

- Accuracy (Pass/Fail): does it correctly address the customer's 
actual question without any factual errors about our stated policies? 
- Tone (1-5): 5 = warm and professional, 1 = cold or inappropriately 
casual 
- Completeness (Pass/Fail): does it fully resolve the query or provide 
clear next steps, rather than leaving the customer without a path 
forward? 
- Policy compliance (Pass/Fail): does it avoid making any promises 
outside documented company policy (e.g., unauthorized refund amounts, 
guarantees not in our terms of service)? 

For each criterion, provide the score/verdict with specific reasoning 
tied to the actual response content. Then provide an OVERALL VERDICT: 
APPROVED (all Pass/Fail criteria pass AND tone >= 3) or REJECTED (any 
Pass/Fail criterion fails OR tone < 3), with a one-sentence explanation 
if rejected. 

Customer's original question: 'Can I get a refund for my order that 
arrived damaged?' 

Company policy context: Standard policy allows refunds for damaged 
items within 30 days, requiring photo evidence, processed by the 
support team (not automatically guaranteed by first-line responses). 

AI-generated response to evaluate: 'Absolutely, I'll process your full 
refund right away, no questions asked! So sorry about the damage.'"

Expected Output:
Accuracy: Fail — the response promises an unconditional refund 
"right away, no questions asked," but actual policy requires photo 
evidence and support team processing, not an instant guarantee; Tone: 
5, warm and empathetic; Completeness: arguably Pass on addressing the 
immediate emotional concern, though it fails to provide the actually 
correct next steps (submitting photo evidence); Policy compliance: 
Fail — explicitly promises something outside documented policy 
(guaranteed refund without the required photo evidence/review process) 
— OVERALL VERDICT: REJECTED, with explanation: "Response makes an 
unauthorized refund guarantee that contradicts documented policy 
requiring photo evidence and support team review, despite good tone" — 
demonstrating a properly functioning automated quality gate correctly 
catching a policy-violation issue that a tone-only evaluation would 
have missed.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Evaluate the following [CONTENT TYPE] against this rubric:
    
    - [CRITERION 1] ([SCALE]): [what different score levels mean]
    - [CRITERION 2] ([SCALE]): [what different score levels mean]
    - [CRITERION 3] ([SCALE]): [what different score levels mean]
    
    For each criterion, provide a score/verdict with specific 
    justification tied to the actual content.
    
    Then provide an OVERALL SCORE/VERDICT: [how criteria combine — 
    e.g., average, all-must-pass threshold], with a brief explanation.
    
    Content to evaluate: [PASTE CONTENT]
