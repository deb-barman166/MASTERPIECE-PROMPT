-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Screenplay Prompting — Generating Properly Formatted Script Content

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Screenplay prompting is a specialized technique for generating content 
in proper SCREENPLAY/SCRIPT FORMAT — the specific industry-standard 
conventions for scene headings (INT./EXT.), action lines, character 
cues, dialogue blocks, and parentheticals — used for film, television, 
or stage writing. This differs from general Story Prompting (file 46) 
in that it requires strict adherence to a specific FORMATTING 
CONVENTION alongside narrative content, since screenplay format itself 
carries functional meaning (it tells a production crew when a scene 
changes, who's speaking, and how something should be performed) — 
directly relevant to filmmaking-focused prompt work like Deb Barman's 
Ultimate Hollywood AI Filmmaking Prompt Library.

Think of it like the difference between writing a short story about a 
conversation (prose, free-flowing) versus writing that same 
conversation as it would appear in an actual shooting script — with 
proper scene headings, character names in caps above their dialogue, 
and action lines describing only what's visually observable (not 
internal thoughts, since a camera can't see those).

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Screenplay prompting exists to:
- Produce genuinely INDUSTRY-FORMATTED script content usable in real 
  pre-production/development contexts, not just narrative prose 
  loosely resembling a script.
- Enforce the discipline of "SHOW, DON'T TELL" that screenwriting 
  fundamentally requires — action lines can only describe what a 
  camera/audience can observe, not internal character thoughts or 
  feelings.
- Support specific FILM/TV/STAGE pre-production workflows: outlining 
  scenes, drafting dialogue, developing specific beats for later 
  human refinement.
- Enable CONSISTENT formatting across a longer script or multiple 
  scenes, critical for actual production use.
- Serve creative pipelines combining screenplay generation with visual 
  content creation (storyboarding, AI video generation prompts derived 
  from scripted scenes).

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Drafting scenes, sequences, or full scripts for film, television, or 
  stage projects.
- Developing dialogue-heavy content that specifically needs proper 
  script formatting for later production use.
- Pre-visualization workflows where a scripted scene will later inform 
  storyboards, shot lists, or AI-generated video content.
- Screenwriting practice, competition submissions, or portfolio 
  development requiring professional format standards.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- General narrative fiction not intended for screen/stage production 
  (use Story Prompting instead).
- Casual dialogue exchanges that don't require formal script 
  conventions (use Dialogue Prompting, file 52, for more general 
  conversational writing).
- Very short content where the formatting overhead isn't warranted 
  (a single joke exchange doesn't need scene headings).

BEST MODELS FOR SCREENPLAY PROMPTING:
- Claude — strong at maintaining consistent screenplay formatting 
  across longer scenes, with good instinct for "camera-observable" 
  action lines rather than prose-style internal narration.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class — capable, widely-used for screenplay drafting, 
  generally reliable format adherence.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra — capable screenplay generation, particularly 
  useful in multimodal pipelines feeding into visual/video generation 
  tools.
- Grok — capable of punchy, dialogue-driven scenes given its 
  conversational strengths.
- Mistral / Llama — capable of basic screenplay format when explicitly 
  shown the convention; may need a concrete formatting example to 
  reliably maintain proper structure, especially in longer outputs.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [SCENE CONTEXT] + "Write in proper screenplay format: INT./EXT. 
    [LOCATION] - [TIME]. Action lines describing only observable 
    action. CHARACTER NAME in caps above dialogue." + [SPECIFIC SCENE 
    GOAL/CONTENT]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Scene Context — what's happening, who's involved, where/when.
    2. Format Instruction — explicit reminder of screenplay 
       conventions (scene heading format, action line rules, dialogue 
       formatting, parenthetical usage).
    3. Character/Tone Guidance — relevant character voice notes if 
       established elsewhere (see Character Prompting, file 48).
    4. Scene Goal — what needs to happen/be revealed/accomplished in 
       this specific scene.
    5. Length/Scope — roughly how long the scene should be (page count 
       is the industry standard measure, roughly 1 page ≈ 1 minute of 
       screen time).

Example skeleton:
    "Write a screenplay scene in proper industry format. 
    
    Scene context: [WHO, WHERE, WHEN, WHAT'S HAPPENING] 
    
    Format requirements: 
    - Scene heading: INT./EXT. LOCATION - TIME 
    - Action lines: describe only what's visually/aurally observable, 
      present tense 
    - Character names in ALL CAPS above their dialogue 
    - Use parentheticals sparingly, only when genuinely needed for 
      clarity 
    
    Scene goal: [WHAT THIS SCENE NEEDS TO ACCOMPLISH] 
    Approximate length: [X pages/minutes]"

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Explicitly enforce the "camera can't see thoughts" rule — action 
  lines should describe OBSERVABLE behavior/expression, not internal 
  states (e.g., "She grips the steering wheel, knuckles white" rather 
  than "She feels anxious").
- Specify PRESENT TENSE for action lines, which is the standard 
  screenplay convention (scripts describe what's happening NOW on 
  screen, not what happened).
- Use parentheticals (brief actor direction under a character's name, 
  before their line) SPARINGLY — overuse is considered poor 
  screenwriting practice; only include when genuinely necessary for 
  clarity that dialogue alone wouldn't convey.
- Keep ACTION LINES CONCISE — professional screenplay convention 
  favors short, punchy action description over dense paragraphs, since 
  page count roughly correlates to screen time.
- For DIALOGUE specifically, remind the model that screen dialogue is 
  typically more clipped/naturalistic than prose dialogue — people 
  interrupt, trail off, and don't speak in complete grammatical 
  paragraphs.
- If you have ESTABLISHED characters (from prior Character Prompting 
  work), provide their voice/personality notes so dialogue stays 
  consistent with who they are.
- For longer scripts, consider scene-by-scene generation with review 
  in between, similar to Plan-and-Solve Prompting (file 24) applied to 
  screenwriting.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Action lines describing internal thoughts/feelings that a 
  camera couldn't actually capture ("He feels a deep sense of dread").
  FIX: Explicitly instruct that action lines must describe only 
  observable behavior, and rewrite internal-state descriptions as 
  physical behavior/expression.

- MISTAKE: Overusing parentheticals under every line of dialogue, 
  which professional screenwriting convention considers poor practice.
  FIX: Reserve parentheticals for genuinely necessary clarification 
  only.

- MISTAKE: Writing dialogue that reads like formal written prose 
  rather than naturalistic spoken language (complete, grammatically 
  perfect sentences with no interruption or trailing off).
  FIX: Explicitly request more naturalistic, clipped dialogue patterns 
  typical of screen writing.

- MISTAKE: Inconsistent scene heading format across multiple scenes 
  (sometimes INT./EXT., sometimes just a location name).
  FIX: Explicitly specify and reinforce the exact scene heading 
  convention throughout.

- MISTAKE: Overly dense, paragraph-style action description instead of 
  the concise, punchy style professional screenplays favor.
  FIX: Explicitly request concise action lines, and note that page 
  length roughly correlates to screen time.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a short screenplay scene (about half a page) in proper format. 
Scene: Two coworkers meet by the office coffee machine. One just found 
out she got promoted; the other is her close friend who was up for the 
same role and didn't get it. Keep dialogue naturalistic and let the 
tension show through action, not exposition."

Expected Output:
A properly formatted scene with an INT. OFFICE - DAY (or similar) 
heading, concise action lines showing observable tension (hesitation, 
a forced smile, avoided eye contact) rather than stated emotion, and 
naturalistic, clipped dialogue that lets the awkwardness emerge through 
subtext rather than characters directly stating their feelings.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a 1-page screenplay scene in proper format. 

Scene context: A cyberpunk thriller. Detective Vasquez interrogates a 
hacker suspect in a dimly lit precinct room. The hacker knows more 
than they're letting on but is trying to appear cooperative. 

Format requirements: 
- INT./EXT. scene heading 
- Concise, observable action lines only 
- Naturalistic, clipped dialogue with subtext (the hacker should imply 
things without directly stating them) 
- Use parentheticals only if truly necessary 

Scene goal: By the end, Vasquez should suspect the hacker is lying 
about something specific, without it being stated outright."

Expected Output:
A properly formatted interrogation scene with appropriate noir/
cyberpunk atmospheric action lines (lighting, physical tells), 
naturalistic dialogue where the hacker's evasions create subtext-
driven tension, and an ending beat that plants suspicion through 
implication (a caught detail, a too-quick answer, a physical tell) 
rather than an on-the-nose statement like "I think he's lying."

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Write a 2-page screenplay scene in proper industry format for a 
sci-fi drama. 

Established characters: 
- DR. AMARA CHEN: xenobiologist, controlled and precise in speech, 
rarely shows emotion outwardly but has a habit of adjusting her glasses 
when stressed. 
- COMMANDER REYES: station commander, blunt, pragmatic, has a history 
of prioritizing crew safety over discovery, creating friction with 
Amara. 

Scene context: Amara has just detected what might be an alien signal, 
but confirming it requires disabling a safety protocol that Reyes 
would never approve. She's trying to convince him without revealing 
her actual plan to bypass his authority if he refuses. 

Format requirements: 
- Proper INT. scene heading 
- Present-tense, observable-only action lines 
- Dialogue should carry significant subtext — Amara is not being 
fully honest, and sharp-eyed audience members should sense this without 
it being stated 
- Reflect each character's established voice/mannerisms (Amara's 
glasses habit should appear at least once, tied to a moment of 
internal conflict) 

Scene goal: Reyes refuses her request, and the scene should end with a 
clear sense that Amara has NOT abandoned her plan, without her saying 
so directly."

Expected Output:
A properly formatted 2-page scene consistent with both characters' 
established voices — Amara speaking with controlled precision while 
her glasses-adjusting habit surfaces at a moment of internal tension 
(likely right before or after a moment of dishonesty/omission), Reyes 
responding with blunt pragmatism reflecting his safety-first history — 
building genuine subtext throughout their exchange, ending with Reyes's 
refusal and a final beat (a look, a small action, a slightly-too-quick 
agreement) that signals to the audience Amara isn't actually done, 
without any dialogue stating this outright — demonstrating sophisticated 
integration of character consistency, subtext, and proper format 
discipline simultaneously.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Write a screenplay scene in proper industry format.
    
    Scene context: [WHO IS INVOLVED, WHERE, WHEN, WHAT'S HAPPENING]
    
    [OPTIONAL: Established character voices/mannerisms: [NOTES]]
    
    Format requirements:
    - Scene heading: INT./EXT. LOCATION - TIME
    - Action lines: present tense, describing only what's 
    visually/aurally observable — no internal thoughts or feelings 
    stated directly
    - CHARACTER NAMES in all caps above dialogue
    - Parentheticals used sparingly, only when truly necessary
    - Naturalistic, clipped dialogue with subtext where appropriate
    
    Scene goal: [WHAT THIS SCENE NEEDS TO ACCOMPLISH DRAMATICALLY]
    Approximate length: [X pages]
