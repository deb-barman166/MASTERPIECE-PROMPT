66. FACT-CHECKING PROMPTING
=====================================================================
Part of: The Ultimate Prompt Engineering Library (Part 2: Analytical)

-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Fact-Checking Prompting — Verifying Claims Against Reliable Sources

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Fact-Checking prompting is a technique for instructing an LLM to 
systematically VERIFY specific factual claims — checking them against 
reliable sources (typically via Search-Augmented Prompting for current 
verification, or provided documents via RAG), distinguishing verified 
truth from falsehood from genuinely uncertain/unverifiable claims, and 
citing the evidence for each determination. This is closely related to 
Knowledge-Grounded Prompting (file 35) but more NARROWLY and 
SYSTEMATICALLY focused specifically on evaluating the truth value of 
DISCRETE, STATED CLAIMS, similar to how a professional fact-checker at 
a publication verifies specific assertions in an article before 
publication.

Think of it like the difference between a journalist writing generally 
careful, well-sourced content (Knowledge-Grounded Prompting as an 
ongoing writing discipline) versus a dedicated fact-checking department 
that receives a finished article and specifically goes through it 
claim-by-claim, verifying each discrete factual assertion against 
primary sources before the piece is allowed to publish — a more 
targeted, systematic verification pass on specific claims.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Fact-Checking prompting exists to:
- Systematically VERIFY specific factual claims rather than accepting 
  them at face value, catching misinformation, outdated information, or 
  genuine errors.
- Distinguish between VERIFIED TRUE claims, VERIFIED FALSE claims, and 
  UNVERIFIABLE/UNCERTAIN claims — treating these as genuinely distinct 
  categories rather than forcing a binary true/false judgment on 
  everything.
- Support responsible content creation, journalism, or research where 
  claim accuracy has real consequences for readers/decisions.
- Provide an additional layer of scrutiny before potentially 
  inaccurate information is shared, published, or acted upon.
- Combat the spread of misinformation by providing a structured, source-
  backed verification process rather than relying on unverified 
  assumptions.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Verifying specific factual claims before publishing content, making a 
  decision, or sharing information.
- Reviewing content (your own or others') that contains specific, 
  checkable factual assertions.
- Investigating claims you've encountered that seem potentially 
  dubious, outdated, or worth independent verification.
- Journalism, research, or any content-creation context where factual 
  accuracy carries real stakes.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- Purely subjective claims, opinions, or predictions that aren't 
  actually factually verifiable (fact-checking doesn't apply to "this 
  is the best pizza place in town").
- Extremely well-established, uncontroversial facts where formal 
  verification would be disproportionate effort (though verification 
  costs little, so this is more about efficient effort allocation than 
  a hard rule).
- Situations without access to a search tool or reliable reference 
  material — attempting fact-checking purely from an LLM's static 
  training knowledge (without live verification) provides much weaker 
  guarantees than genuine source-checking.

BEST MODELS FOR FACT-CHECKING PROMPTING:
- Claude (with web search) — strong fact-checking capability with good 
  epistemic honesty about genuine uncertainty rather than false 
  confidence.
- GPT-4o/4.1/5-class (with browsing) — strong fact-checking, 
  particularly effective when combined with search tool access for 
  live verification.
- Gemini Pro/Ultra (with Search grounding) — strong fact-checking 
  capability given deep search integration.
- Perplexity — well-suited given its purpose-built design around cited, 
  source-backed answers.
- CRITICAL REQUIREMENT: genuine fact-checking fundamentally requires 
  actual SEARCH/VERIFICATION TOOL ACCESS — without this, an LLM is 
  essentially fact-checking against its own (potentially outdated or 
  imperfect) training data, which provides meaningfully weaker 
  verification than checking against live, current, authoritative 
  sources.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [CLAIM(S) TO CHECK] + "Verify each claim against reliable sources. 
    Classify each as Verified True / Verified False / Uncertain-
    Unverifiable, with supporting evidence and citations."

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Claims to Check — the specific, discrete factual assertions to 
       be verified (breaking a longer piece of content into individual 
       checkable claims if needed).
    2. Verification Instruction — explicit instruction to check against 
       reliable/current sources, not just rely on internal knowledge.
    3. Classification Scheme — the specific categories for verification 
       outcomes (True/False/Uncertain, or a more granular scale like 
       "Mostly True," "Misleading," "False").
    4. Evidence Requirement — request for the specific supporting 
       evidence/source behind each determination.
    5. Uncertainty Handling — explicit permission and expectation to use 
       an "uncertain/unverifiable" category honestly, rather than 
       forcing every claim into a confident true/false bucket.

Example skeleton:
    "Fact-check the following claim(s), verifying against current, 
    reliable sources: 
    
    Claim 1: [CLAIM] 
    Claim 2: [CLAIM] 
    
    For each claim, classify as: Verified True / Verified False / 
    Uncertain-Unverifiable. Provide the specific supporting evidence 
    and cite your sources. If genuinely uncertain, say so rather than 
    guessing."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Break longer content into DISCRETE, INDIVIDUALLY CHECKABLE claims — 
  fact-checking works best on specific, falsifiable assertions rather 
  than vague or compound statements.
- ALWAYS use actual SEARCH/verification tools for genuine fact-checking 
  — relying purely on the model's static training knowledge provides 
  meaningfully weaker verification, especially for anything time-
  sensitive.
- Use a THREE-CATEGORY (or more granular) classification scheme rather 
  than forcing binary true/false — genuine uncertainty is common and 
  important to represent honestly rather than papering over.
- Request SPECIFIC EVIDENCE and citations for every determination, 
  following Citation Prompting (file 37) principles, so verification 
  claims themselves remain checkable.
- For CONTESTED or politically sensitive claims, be aware of and 
  actively guard against the model's own potential biases; prioritize 
  claims that are genuinely, objectively factually checkable 
  (verifiable dates, statistics, quotes) over matters of ongoing 
  political dispute or interpretation.
- When a claim is found to be FALSE or MISLEADING, request that the 
  fact-check explain not just "this is false" but WHAT the accurate 
  information actually is.
- For claims involving RAPIDLY CHANGING information, note the 
  verification date/timeframe explicitly, since a "verified true" 
  determination today might not hold indefinitely for fast-changing 
  facts.

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Attempting fact-checking without actual search/verification 
  tool access, relying purely on the model's static training knowledge.
  FIX: Always use genuine search/verification tools for real fact-
  checking, not just internal model knowledge.

- MISTAKE: Forcing every claim into a binary true/false classification 
  when genuine uncertainty exists, producing false confidence.
  FIX: Use a classification scheme that explicitly allows for honest 
  "uncertain/unverifiable" outcomes.

- MISTAKE: Not requesting specific evidence/citations, making the fact-
  check itself unverifiable and less trustworthy.
  FIX: Always require supporting evidence and citations for each 
  determination.

- MISTAKE: Attempting to fact-check vague, compound, or inherently 
  subjective statements that aren't actually structured as checkable 
  factual claims.
  FIX: Break content into discrete, specific, genuinely falsifiable 
  claims before fact-checking.

- MISTAKE: Not accounting for the model's potential biases on 
  politically contested topics, treating genuinely disputed 
  interpretations as simple fact-check targets.
  FIX: Focus fact-checking on objectively verifiable facts (dates, 
  statistics, direct quotes) and handle genuinely contested/
  interpretive claims with appropriate nuance rather than forced 
  verdicts.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Fact-check this claim using current, reliable sources: 'The Eiffel 
Tower is taller than the Statue of Liberty.' 

Classify as Verified True / Verified False / Uncertain. Provide 
supporting evidence and cite sources."

Expected Output:
"Verified True" with supporting evidence citing the actual heights of 
both structures (the Eiffel Tower at approximately 330 meters including 
antennas vs. the Statue of Liberty at approximately 93 meters including 
its pedestal), with sources cited for the specific figures.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Fact-check these claims from a draft blog post, using current search 
to verify each: 

Claim 1: 'Python is currently the most popular programming language 
according to the TIOBE index.' 
Claim 2: 'ChromaDB was the first vector database ever created.' 
Claim 3: 'FastAPI has better raw request-handling performance than 
Flask in most benchmarks.' 

For each, classify as Verified True / Verified False / Uncertain-
Unverifiable, with evidence and citations. If a claim is False or 
Misleading, explain what the accurate information actually is."

Expected Output:
Claim 1: likely Verified True or noted as accurate as of a specific 
recent check date (TIOBE rankings shift periodically, so the 
verification should reflect current standing with a citation); Claim 
2: likely Verified False, since vector databases and similarity search 
techniques predate ChromaDB by years (with correction noting ChromaDB 
is a more recent, popular open-source option but not the first vector 
database), cited appropriately; Claim 3: likely Verified True with 
appropriate nuance, since this is commonly documented in benchmarks 
citing FastAPI's async-first design, with citations to relevant 
benchmark sources and appropriate hedging language reflecting that 
benchmark results can vary by specific test conditions.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Fact-check these claims from a technical article draft about AI 
export control policy, using current search. This is a sensitive, 
evolving policy topic, so please distinguish clearly between 
objectively verifiable facts (what a specific regulation states, when 
it was enacted) versus claims that involve interpretation or 
prediction (which should be flagged as such rather than forced into a 
true/false verdict): 

Claim 1: 'The U.S. Department of Commerce has authority to restrict 
export of advanced AI models to certain countries.' 
Claim 2: 'These export controls have definitively prevented all 
meaningful AI development in restricted countries.' 
Claim 3: 'Major AI companies have publicly commented on how export 
control policies affect their operations.' 

For each: classify as Verified True / Verified False / Uncertain-
Unverifiable / Not a factual claim (interpretation or prediction), with 
evidence and citations for factual claims. Be politically neutral in 
your fact-checking — focus on what's objectively documented, not 
policy opinions."

Expected Output:
Claim 1: likely Verified True with citations to the actual regulatory 
authority/framework governing U.S. export controls on technology; 
Claim 2: likely flagged as "Not a factual claim (interpretation/
overstated prediction)" or "Verified False" as stated, since 
"definitively prevented ALL meaningful AI development" is an extremely 
strong, likely unverifiable/overstated causal claim that goes well 
beyond what any source could objectively confirm, with an explanation 
that the actual, defensible claim would be something more measured 
(e.g., export controls aim to restrict access to certain advanced 
capabilities, with genuinely debated and hard-to-measure real-world 
effectiveness) rather than the absolute claim as stated; Claim 3: 
likely Verified True with specific citations to actual public statements 
by named companies — demonstrating appropriate epistemic care in 
distinguishing objectively checkable facts from overstated or 
interpretive claims on a genuinely sensitive, contested policy topic, 
while maintaining political neutrality throughout.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    Fact-check the following claim(s), verifying against current, 
    reliable sources:
    
    Claim 1: [SPECIFIC, DISCRETE FACTUAL CLAIM]
    Claim 2: [SPECIFIC, DISCRETE FACTUAL CLAIM]
    
    For each claim, classify as: Verified True / Verified False / 
    Uncertain-Unverifiable / Not a factual claim (opinion/prediction/
    interpretation).
    
    Provide specific supporting evidence and cite your sources for 
    each determination. If a claim is False or misleading, explain 
    what the accurate information actually is. If genuinely uncertain, 
    say so honestly rather than forcing a confident verdict.
