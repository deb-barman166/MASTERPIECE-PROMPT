-----------------------------
1. TITLE
-----------------------------
Video Prompting — Crafting Effective Prompts for AI Video Generation

-----------------------------
2. DEFINITION
-----------------------------
Video prompting is the technique of writing text prompts specifically 
optimized for TEXT-TO-VIDEO and IMAGE-TO-VIDEO generation models (Sora, 
Runway Gen-3/Gen-4, Kling, Luma Dream Machine, Google Veo, Pika) — 
extending Image Prompting (file 48) with the additional dimension of 
TIME: camera movement, subject motion, scene duration, pacing, and 
temporal consistency across frames. Video generation models need to be 
told not just what a scene LOOKS like, but how it MOVES and CHANGES 
over the clip's duration, which introduces an entirely new set of 
prompting considerations beyond static image description.

Think of it like the difference between briefing a photographer 
(single frozen moment — Image Prompting) versus briefing a cinema-
tographer and director together (an entire shot: what the camera does, 
what the subject does, how the moment evolves from first frame to 
last) — video prompting requires specifying that temporal, 
directorial dimension explicitly.

-----------------------------
3. PURPOSE
-----------------------------
Video prompting exists to:
- Communicate both VISUAL and TEMPORAL/MOTION intent to video 
  generation models, which is fundamentally more complex than static 
  image description.
- Control CAMERA MOVEMENT (pan, tilt, zoom, dolly, static) and SUBJECT 
  MOTION independently, since these interact to create the final 
  perceived shot.
- Achieve appropriate PACING and duration for the intended use case 
  (a quick social media clip vs. a slower, more contemplative shot).
- Support emerging AI FILMMAKING workflows where generated video clips 
  need to match a specific creative vision closely enough to be usable 
  in a larger edited piece — directly relevant to AI filmmaking 
  pipelines and prompt libraries for cinematic content generation.
- Bridge traditional film/cinematography vocabulary (shot types, 
  camera moves, lighting) with the specific technical response 
  patterns of current video generation models.

-----------------------------
4. WHEN TO USE AND BEST MODEL OR LLM
-----------------------------
WHEN TO USE:
- Generating short AI video clips for creative projects, social media 
  content, previsualization, or experimental filmmaking.
- Extending a still image into motion (image-to-video workflows).
- Storyboarding or pre-visualizing a scene before committing to full 
  production resources.
- Any content where MOTION itself (not just a static composition) is 
  the point — a product rotating, a character walking, weather 
  effects, camera reveals.

WHEN NOT TO USE:
- When a static image would fully serve the creative need (use Image 
  Prompting instead — video generation is more computationally 
  expensive and technically limited).
- Precise, long-form narrative video requiring exact continuity across 
  many minutes — current video generation models excel at short clips 
  (seconds) and struggle with long-duration consistency.
- Content requiring exact, literal reproduction of real, specific, 
  identifiable people or copyrighted characters.

BEST MODELS/TOOLS FOR VIDEO PROMPTING:
- Runway Gen-3/Gen-4 — strong general-purpose text-to-video and 
  image-to-video, with good camera motion control vocabulary support.
- Google Veo — strong cinematic quality and physics realism, 
  particularly good at complex camera movements and longer, more 
  coherent clips.
- Kling — strong for realistic human motion and longer clip durations 
  compared to some competitors.
- Luma Dream Machine — accessible, good general quality, useful for 
  quick iterative concept generation.
- Pika — known for stylistic flexibility and specific creative effects 
  (like Pikaffects for object transformations).
- OpenAI Sora — strong for complex multi-subject scenes and longer 
  coherent narrative sequences, when available.
- General LLMs (Claude, GPT, Gemini) are frequently used as prompt-
  writing assistants to help expand a simple creative idea into a 
  detailed, well-structured video generation prompt optimized for a 
  specific target tool.

-----------------------------
5. PROMPT FORMULA
-----------------------------
    [SUBJECT] + [SUBJECT MOTION/ACTION] + [SETTING] + [CAMERA 
    MOVEMENT] + [SHOT TYPE] + [STYLE] + [LIGHTING] + [PACING/DURATION 
    CUE] + [MOOD]

-----------------------------
6. PROMPT STRUCTURE
-----------------------------
    1. Subject — the main focal point of the video.
    2. Subject Motion — what the subject does across the clip's 
       duration (walks forward, turns to look, transforms, grows).
    3. Setting/Environment — the background/location context.
    4. Camera Movement — static, slow pan, dolly-in, aerial tracking 
       shot, handheld shake, orbit.
    5. Shot Type — wide shot, close-up, medium shot, extreme close-up.
    6. Style/Medium — cinematic, photorealistic, anime, stop-motion 
       aesthetic.
    7. Lighting — consistent with Image Prompting conventions but 
       consider how it might shift/move during the clip.
    8. Pacing Cue — slow and contemplative, fast and energetic, 
       building tension.
    9. Mood/Atmosphere — the emotional tone.

Example skeleton:
    "[SHOT TYPE] of [SUBJECT] [SUBJECT MOTION], [SETTING]. Camera: 
    [CAMERA MOVEMENT]. Style: [STYLE/MEDIUM]. Lighting: [LIGHTING]. 
    [PACING/MOOD DESCRIPTION]."

-----------------------------
7. BEST PRACTICES
-----------------------------
- Describe MOTION explicitly and specifically for BOTH the subject and 
  the camera — these are two independent variables, and conflating 
  them (only describing one) leaves the other to chance.
- Use established CINEMATOGRAPHY vocabulary (dolly-in, tracking shot, 
  Dutch angle, rack focus) since these terms carry precise, well-
  understood meaning that video models are often specifically trained 
  to recognize.
- Keep individual clip prompts FOCUSED on a single continuous action/
  movement — current video generation excels at short (a few seconds 
  to ~10-20 seconds depending on tool), coherent movements rather than 
  complex multi-beat sequences within one generation.
- Specify PACING explicitly ("slow, deliberate movement" vs. "quick, 
  energetic motion") since this significantly affects the felt quality 
  of the generated clip beyond just the visual content.
- For IMAGE-TO-VIDEO workflows, ensure your starting image already has 
  good composition/lighting, since the video model will animate FROM 
  that starting point — a flawed starting image constrains the 
  possible output quality.
- Understand your TOOL's specific duration/technical limitations 
  (maximum clip length, resolution, aspect ratio support) before 
  designing your creative shot list around assumptions that might not 
  hold.
- For MULTI-SHOT sequences building toward a larger edited piece, plan 
  each shot's prompt with awareness of how it will cut together with 
  adjacent shots (consistent style/lighting/color direction across the 
  sequence).

-----------------------------
8. COMMON MISTAKES
-----------------------------
- MISTAKE: Describing only the subject's visual appearance without 
  specifying either subject motion or camera movement, leaving the 
  temporal dimension entirely to chance.
  FIX: Always explicitly describe both what the subject does and what 
  the camera does across the clip's duration.

- MISTAKE: Attempting to pack a complex, multi-beat narrative sequence 
  into a single short video generation, when current tools handle 
  focused, single continuous actions far more reliably.
  FIX: Break complex sequences into multiple shorter clips, each with 
  a focused single action/movement.

- MISTAKE: Not using precise cinematography vocabulary, instead using 
  vague descriptions ("camera moves around") that don't give the model 
  clear direction.
  FIX: Use specific, established camera movement terminology.

- MISTAKE: Starting an image-to-video generation from a poorly 
  composed or low-quality source image and expecting the video model 
  to compensate.
  FIX: Ensure the starting image already has strong composition and 
  quality before animating it.

- MISTAKE: Not planning for visual/stylistic consistency across a 
  multi-shot sequence intended to be edited together, resulting in 
  clips that don't cut together cohesively.
  FIX: Maintain consistent style, lighting, and color direction 
  language across all prompts in a planned sequence.

-----------------------------
9. EXAMPLES (BASIC, INTERMEDIATE, ADVANCED)
-----------------------------

--- BASIC EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Wide shot of a hot air balloon slowly rising above a misty mountain 
valley at dawn. Camera: static, held steady. Style: photorealistic, 
cinematic. Lighting: soft golden dawn light breaking through mist. 
Pacing: slow, peaceful, contemplative."

Expected Output:
A short AI-generated video clip showing a hot air balloon gently 
ascending through misty mountain scenery, filmed from a fixed, steady 
camera position, with warm dawn lighting filtering through atmospheric 
mist, moving at an unhurried, serene pace matching the specified mood.

--- INTERMEDIATE EXAMPLE ---
Prompt:
"Medium tracking shot following a cyberpunk courier riding a hover-
bike through a rain-slicked neon-lit alley at night. Subject motion: 
weaving smoothly between obstacles, jacket flapping in the wind. 
Camera: smooth tracking shot moving alongside the subject at 
consistent speed, slight motion blur on background elements. Style: 
cinematic digital art, Blade Runner-inspired. Lighting: neon reflections 
on wet pavement, magenta and cyan color grade. Pacing: fast, energetic, 
building momentum."

Expected Output:
A dynamic clip depicting the courier's hoverbike moving through the 
alley with the camera smoothly tracking alongside at matching speed, 
appropriate motion blur suggesting velocity on background elements, 
consistent neon-reflective wet-pavement aesthetic with the specified 
magenta/cyan color grade, and an energetic pace matching the 
described fast, momentum-building feel.

--- ADVANCED EXAMPLE ---
Prompt (as part of a planned 3-shot sequence for a short AI film, 
maintaining visual consistency):
"Shot 2 of 3 in sequence — maintain consistent teal/orange cinematic 
color grade and overcast lighting conditions established in Shot 1 
(a wide establishing shot of an abandoned space station exterior). 

Close-up shot: an astronaut's gloved hand reaching toward a 
flickering control panel inside the station's dim interior. Subject 
motion: hand moves slowly, deliberately, hesitating slightly before 
making contact with a specific button, which sparks briefly on 
contact. Camera: slow push-in (dolly-in) toward the hand, rack focus 
shifting from the background control panel lights to sharp focus on 
the hand as it gets closer. Style: photorealistic, matching Shot 1's 
grounded sci-fi aesthetic (not stylized/animated). Lighting: dim, 
flickering emergency lighting, consistent teal-orange color grade 
established in the sequence. Pacing: slow, tense, deliberate — 
building anticipation for a cut to Shot 3."

Expected Output:
A close-up clip showing an astronaut's gloved hand slowly, hesitantly 
reaching toward a control panel, the camera performing a smooth dolly-
in with a rack focus shift from background to the hand, a small spark 
effect when contact is made, all rendered in a photorealistic style 
matching the described teal-orange color grade and dim emergency 
lighting established as consistent with a prior shot in the sequence, 
paced slowly to build tension — demonstrating the kind of sequence-
aware, continuity-conscious prompting needed for multi-shot AI 
filmmaking projects where individual clips must cut together cohesively 
into a larger edited piece.

-----------------------------
10. REUSABLE PROMPT TEMPLATE
-----------------------------
    [SHOT TYPE] of [SUBJECT] [SUBJECT MOTION/ACTION ACROSS THE CLIP].
    
    Setting: [ENVIRONMENT/BACKGROUND]
    
    Camera: [CAMERA MOVEMENT — static/pan/dolly-in/tracking/orbit/
    handheld]
    
    Style: [VISUAL STYLE/MEDIUM]
    
    Lighting: [LIGHTING DESCRIPTION, noting any changes across the 
    clip]
    
    Pacing: [SLOW/FAST, MOOD-APPROPRIATE MOTION SPEED]
    
    [IF PART OF A SEQUENCE: Maintain consistency with [PRIOR SHOT'S 
    color grade/lighting/style] established in [Shot X].]
