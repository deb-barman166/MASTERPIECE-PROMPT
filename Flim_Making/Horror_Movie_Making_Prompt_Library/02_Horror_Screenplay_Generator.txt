1. TITLE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
HORROR SCREENPLAY GENERATOR
Category: Script Development
Compatible Engines: GPT-4/5, Claude, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek, and future LLMs

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
2. PURPOSE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Converts a story/treatment (ideally from Template 01) into an industry-
standard horror screenplay in proper format — sluglines, action lines,
dialogue blocks, parentheticals — while encoding cinematic timing directly
into the prose. Horror screenwriting differs from general screenwriting in
one critical way: PACING IS THE PRODUCT. This template forces explicit
control over beat length, white space, and silence so the script reads with
the same tension it's meant to produce on screen.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
3. PROMPT FORMULA
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
[FORMAT LOCK] + [SOURCE MATERIAL] + [SCENE LIST WITH SLUGLINES] +
[PACING RULE SET] + [SUBTEXT LAYER PER SCENE] + [SOUND CUE INTEGRATION] +
[CHARACTER VOICE PROFILES] + [WHITE SPACE DIRECTIVE] + [OUTPUT FORMAT LOCK]

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
4. VARIABLES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
{SOURCE_MATERIAL} — story/treatment text or logline+beats to adapt
{SCENE_COUNT} — number of scenes to script in this pass
{FORMAT_STANDARD} — US Feature Standard / Netflix Style Guide / Short Film
{PACING_PROFILE} — slow-burn (long action lines, sparse dialogue) vs.
  kinetic (short lines, rapid cuts implied)
{SUBTEXT_LAYER} — what each character is NOT saying that the audience should
  sense (per major scene)
{DIALOGUE_DENSITY} — minimal (horror often needs less talk, more behavior)
  / moderate / dialogue-driven
{CHARACTER_VOICE_PROFILE} — per named character: speech rhythm, vocabulary
  level, verbal tics, what they never say
{SOUND_CUE_STYLE} — inline (SOUND CUE: ...) vs. integrated into action lines
{PAGE_TARGET} — approximate page count (1 page ≈ 1 minute of screen time)
{RATING_TARGET} — R / Unrated / TV-14 equivalent, for gore/language ceiling

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
5. INPUT STRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
You are a professional horror screenwriter formatting to {FORMAT_STANDARD}.

SOURCE MATERIAL:
{SOURCE_MATERIAL}

Convert this into {SCENE_COUNT} fully formatted screenplay scenes.

PACING PROFILE: {PACING_PROFILE}
DIALOGUE DENSITY: {DIALOGUE_DENSITY}
SOUND CUE STYLE: {SOUND_CUE_STYLE}
RATING TARGET: {RATING_TARGET}
PAGE TARGET: {PAGE_TARGET}

CHARACTER VOICE PROFILES:
{CHARACTER_VOICE_PROFILE}

For each scene, before writing it, internally identify:
- The SUBTEXT_LAYER (what's unsaid) driving the scene
- The single moment of maximum tension and where it falls in the scene
  (front-loaded / mid-scene / final beat)
- Whether this scene is a BUILD, RELEASE, or FALSE RELEASE beat

Formatting rules:
- Standard screenplay format: INT./EXT. SLUGLINE - TIME, action lines in
  present tense, character names in caps on first introduction, dialogue
  centered under character cues.
- Action lines describing dread must be SHORT. One idea per line. Horror
  scripts breathe through white space — never write a dense action
  paragraph during a tension beat.
- Use silence explicitly: write "(silence)" or a beat of pure action-line
  description with no dialogue when the scene calls for it. Do not fill
  silence with unnecessary dialogue.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
6. OUTPUT STRUCTURE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
1. SCENE BREAKDOWN TABLE (scene # | slugline | beat type: build/release/
   false-release | subtext layer | est. page length)
2. FULL FORMATTED SCREENPLAY (industry standard formatting)
3. PRODUCTION NOTES (per scene: recommended pacing on set, one line on the
   intended audience physiological response — held breath, dread, relief)

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
7. PROFESSIONAL INSTRUCTIONS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- Action lines are the horror screenwriter's primary tool — dialogue is
  secondary. Write what the camera sees, not what characters feel.
- Withhold character interiority; a horror script shows fear through
  behavior described in action lines (a hand that won't stop checking a
  lock) never through parentheticals like "(terrified)."
- Cut dialogue that explains what the audience can already infer from
  action. If a line can be deleted without losing information, delete it.
- Use ellipses and interruptions in dialogue to convey unspoken dread —
  characters in horror rarely finish sentences about the thing they fear.
- Sluglines should imply mood through specificity: "INT. STAIRWELL — NIGHT"
  is generic; "INT. STAIRWELL, THIRD LANDING — NIGHT, POWER OUT" plants
  atmosphere in the format itself.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
8. BEST PRACTICES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- One scare per scene, maximum, unless explicitly writing a climax
  sequence — stacking scares in one scene dilutes both.
- Write the "wrong" version of a normal action once per act (a character
  reaching for a light switch that isn't where it should be) as a low-cost,
  high-effect unease generator.
- Keep parentheticals almost entirely absent — reserve them only for
  genuinely ambiguous line readings, never for emotion the dialogue already
  conveys.
- End scenes on the image, not on dialogue, whenever possible — horror
  scripts should cut on visual dread, not on a spoken line.
- Vary scene length deliberately: a 1/8-page scene after three long scenes
  creates rhythmic disruption that mirrors anxiety.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
9. HORROR CINEMATIC TECHNIQUES
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- THE HELD SHOT IMPLICATION: write action lines that imply a static camera
  holding on stillness longer than comfortable ("The hallway stays empty.
  It stays empty. It stays empty a beat too long.").
- OFF-SCREEN GEOGRAPHY: establish where the threat is NOT, explicitly, in
  action lines, so its later appearance in that "safe" space lands harder.
- THE FALSE RELEASE SCENE: script one scene per act that resolves like a
  release beat but plants a small, easy-to-miss wrongness in its final line
  of action.
- DIALOGUE UNDERCUT: write one scene of mundane dialogue running
  concurrently with an action line revealing something the characters
  cannot see, creating dramatic irony as dread engine.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
10. CAMERA & LIGHTING GUIDE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- Embed minimal camera direction ONLY when essential to the scare (a script
  should mostly trust the director, but horror scripts earn exceptions):
  use sparingly, e.g., "The camera doesn't move. Neither does she."
- Cross-reference Template 07 (Camera Master) for full shot lists per scene
  once this screenplay pass is locked — this template intentionally
  under-specifies camera to preserve screenwriter/director separation of
  concerns.
- Flag lighting-dependent scenes (e.g., a scene that ONLY works in total
  darkness) with a bracketed production note for Template 09 continuity.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
11. SOUND & ATMOSPHERE GUIDE
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
- Use {SOUND_CUE_STYLE} consistently across the entire script — never mix
  inline SOUND CUE tags with embedded-in-action-line sound description in
  the same draft.
- Mark at least one "SILENCE CUE" per act explicitly — silence must be
  scripted, not assumed, or it will be scored over in post.
- Flag the antagonist's sound motif (inherited from Template 01) at every
  scene where it appears, using a consistent tag for continuity tracking.

13. EXAMPLE OUTPUT (Formatted Excerpt)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SCENE BREAKDOWN:
Scene 1 | INT. RECORD HOUSE, BACK SHELF — NIGHT | BUILD | Subtext: Ines
believes she's found evidence to escape; the scene is actually confirming
she was chosen before she chose | Est. 2 pages

INT. RECORD HOUSE, BACK SHELF — NIGHT

Dust. A single bulb. INES kneels at a shelf of leather ledgers, flashlight
in her teeth.

She finds the newest one. Flips to the back page.

Her name. Today's date.

She flips back a page. Her name again. A date three years ago.

SOUND CUE: the low three-beat hum, distant, once.

She flips back further. And further.

INES
(barely audible)
Okay. Okay, that's —

She doesn't finish it.

Her name. Ten years ago. She would have been eleven.

The bulb flickers once. Doesn't go out.

She closes the ledger very slowly, like it's asleep and she doesn't want
to wake it.

PRODUCTION NOTE: Held-breath beat. No score under this scene — the hum is
the only sound permitted. Actor should physically shrink rather than
startle; the horror here is dawning, not sudden.
